# Act 1 — The World As It Is
## Lecture 1: History and Understanding

Dramatic question: what is this thing I live inside?
Final image: standing at the shore.
Runtime: 60 minutes, ~28 slides.

Internal three acts:
- Setup (0–10): the naive lens — orgs explained by good and bad people.
- Turn (10–45): history and theory break the naive lens.
- Resolution (45–60): this is not cynicism, it is relief. The org is weather.

---

## Beat outline

### Cold open — the quote (5 min, slides 1–3)
1. Title slide: "The Ocean and the Mountain — Corporate Power, Part 1: The World As It Is."
2. The Annie Leonhart quote, alone on a slide. (Attack on Titan, S1; fan-transcribed, one version used consistently.)
3. Context beat: she says this to cadets who just watched the Military Police rot up close. Two explanations on one slide — moral (bad people ruined a good org) vs. structural (the setup reveals what people reliably do). Commit to the structural lens for the series.

### Part 1 — The corporation was never designed (15 min, slides 4–10)
4. English East India Company 1600, Dutch VOC 1602 — corporations as charters, not blueprints.
5. The patchwork history (Gelderblom, De Jong, Jonker): permanent capital arrived 1612, director limited liability 1623 — each feature patched in to fix a crisis. "Legal form followed economic function, not the other way around."
6. Point slide: structures evolve under pressure — usually pressure about capital and control, never about human goodness.
7. Alfred Chandler, The Visible Hand: salaried managers replace owners; separation of ownership and control.
8. The M-form (multi-divisional structure) — General Motors, DuPont, Sloan. Divisions as internal nations.
9. The ownership/control gap is where corporate politics lives — nobody runs the machine for the reasons the machine claims.
10. Modern scale example (anonymized, from public filings): two businesses, four reportable segments, ~390,000 employees, execs rotated between units. What that shape tells you.

### Part 2 — Why hierarchy always re-forms (15 min, slides 11–18)
11. Robert Michels 1911, Political Parties: "Who says organization, says oligarchy."
12. The iron law: delegation creates specialists, specialists entrench. Even orgs founded to prevent hierarchy become hierarchical.
13. Max Weber: the iron cage of bureaucracy — rule by files, by rules, by position.
14. Han Feizi, two thousand years earlier, from the Chinese side: don't trust the minister's heart, read the position he stands in. The oldest structuralist in the room.
15. Conway's Law (Melvin Conway, 1968): organizations that design systems are constrained to produce designs that are copies of the communication structures of those organizations. The Annie thesis in engineering terms — structure doesn't just reveal what people are, it determines what they can produce.
16. Conway is testable in a way Michels never was: Vaish, Gajos & Manku (Microsoft Research, 2017) predicted real org structures from GitHub commit data alone. The thing you shipped is a fossil record of the org.
17. Synthesis slide: four traditions, one finding — hierarchy re-forms because that is what the structure selects for.
18. The Annie line, restated academically: the structure is the evidence of human nature.

### Part 3 — Structure as an X-ray of values (10 min, slides 19–22)
19. How to read what an org actually believes: what gets measured, what gets promoted, how units are split, where budget authority sits.
20. Example: a company split into competing reportable segments believes internal competition produces accountability.
21. Example: a company that rotates execs through units believes loyalty is to the system, not the unit.
22. Exercise prompt: your org's X-ray — three questions to ask this week.

### Part 4 — The emotional turn (12 min, slides 23–27)
23. The objection: isn't this bleak? Isn't this giving up?
24. The turn: it isn't cynicism, it's relief. Nothing feels like betrayal once you read structure. A wave isn't malicious. A landslide isn't personal. You stop being sad at the mountain for being a mountain.
25. Marcus Aurelius 4.3 — the quietest retreat is the mind of the person who works well with what lies in their path. The calm isn't escaping to the mountain; it's the disposition you bring to the meeting room.
26. You can't fight the organization, and you don't have to be sad about it. You just gotta go. (Stoics: the impediment to action advances action — 5.20.)
27. Bridge to Act 2: next time, we learn to read the specific terrain — and we'll borrow a culture that names out loud what ours hides.

### Close (3 min, slide 28)
28. The Annie quote again — now earned. Homework: pick one decision at your company you believe happened in a meeting. Find where it actually happened.
