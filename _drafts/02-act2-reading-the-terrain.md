# Act 2 — Reading the Terrain
## Lecture 2: How Power Actually Works

Dramatic question: where does power actually live, in this specific organization?
Final image: the map.
Runtime: 60 minutes, ~27 slides.

Internal three acts:
- Setup (0–10): the org chart you're given vs. the org that exists.
- Turn (10–40): Japan names out loud what Western orgs hide — the two-layer machine.
- Resolution (40–60): diagnostics — the audience can now point at where a decision really happened.

---

## Beat outline

### Cold open — two org charts (5 min, slides 1–3)
1. Title slide: "Part 2: Reading the Terrain."
2. Two slides side by side: the org chart from the intranet, and a hand-drawn second chart — who talks to whom, who eats lunch with whom, whose approval actually matters. Same company, two machines.
3. Thesis: every formal org has an informal org running underneath it. The informal one decides. This is not corruption — it's physics.

### Part 1 — The mirror: Japan says it out loud (20 min, slides 4–13)
4. Why Japan is the centerpiece: Japanese business vocabulary names the two layers explicitly. Western orgs run the same layers but call them "politics" and feel guilty about it.
5. Ringi-seido: the document with everyone's seal climbing the hierarchy to the president. Looks like the decision process. It isn't.
6. The killer line from the research: "the actual ringi is less about finding a decision than about confirming it."
7. Nemawashi — literally gardening: digging around the roots before transplanting, so the tree survives the move. The decision happens months earlier, one-on-one, off the record. The meeting is the receipt.
8. Honne and tatemae: true feeling and public position. Two layers, culturally licensed. Every org runs both; only Japan has words for them.
9. Senpai / kohai / doki: the informal seniority hierarchy running parallel to the job hierarchy. Age and cohort as weather, not injustice.
10. The reframe: Western execs call nemawashi "lobbying" and treat it as dirty. In the Japanese frame it's maintenance — tending a living system with the seasons.
11. What this costs and buys: slower decisions, faster implementation. Nobody sabotages what they were consulted on.
12. The ringi document is a physical Conway object: its routing path — whose seal, in what order — is a literal record of the communication structure. This ties the Japan half to the diagnostics half: same law, two vocabularies.
13. Synthesis: the formal chart is the tatemae of the org. The informal network is its honne. Neither is fake.

### Part 2 — Diagnostics: how to map your own terrain (22 min, slides 14–23)
14. Diagnostic 1 — follow the budget. Who signs, who can stop, who reallocates without asking. Budget authority is the only org chart that never lies.
15. Diagnostic 2 — follow the information. Who gets the deck before the meeting. Who's on the distribution list. Who's CC'd late.
16. Diagnostic 3 — follow the promotions. Who gets promoted and fast is the org's revealed religion. (Annie's method, applied.)
17. Diagnostic 4 — find the pre-meeting. Every important meeting has a nemawashi phase. Find where it happens: the 1:1, the group chat, the hallway.
18. Diagnostic 5 — meeting mechanics: who speaks first, who speaks last, who summarizes, who nobody contradicts. The last speaker usually owns the meeting.
19. Diagnostic 6 — the calendar. Access to time is access to power. Who can get 30 minutes, and how fast.
20. Diagnostic 7 — the brokers. The people who connect groups that don't normally talk. Structural holes (Burt) — power lives in the gaps between clusters.
21. Diagnostic 8 — follow the artifact (Conway's Law as a tool). The shape of what the company actually ships is the honne: product boundaries, where the interfaces are, which integrations are held together by one person, which teams' work has to be manually reconciled. The most empirical diagnostic — it needs no access, no interviews, and nobody can lie to you about it.
22. Worked example: trace one real decision end-to-end using the eight diagnostics, starting from the artifact and working back to the people. (Anonymized or historical — e.g., a well-documented public decision.)
23. Practice slide: the audience maps one decision from their own week.

### Part 3 — What the map is for (8 min, slides 24–26)
24. The map is not for manipulation. It's for navigation — you can't sail a current you can't see.
25. What reading terrain actually feels like: less surprise, less offense, fewer ambushes. You show up where the decision is being made, not where the calendar says it is.
26. Homework from Act 1 debrief: where did your decision actually happen?

### Close (3 min, slide 27)
27. Final image: the map. Next time — given the terrain, how do you move? Act 3: Moving Well.
