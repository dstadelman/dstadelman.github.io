# The Ocean and the Mountain
## Understanding and Navigating Corporate Power
### A three-part lecture series (3 x 60 minutes)

## The framing (canonical — everything else derives from this)

Three acts for the series, one per lecture. The arc is: see the world, read the terrain, move through it.

**Act 1 — The World As It Is (Lecture 1: History and Understanding)**
Dramatic question: what is this thing I live inside?
The audience starts naive — they explain org behavior through good and bad people. The Annie quote breaks that. History shows the corporation was never designed for human goodness; it was patched together under pressure about capital and control (VOC, Chandler, the M-form). Michels and Weber show hierarchy re-forms even in orgs founded to prevent it. Han Feizi saw the same from the Chinese side two thousand years ago. The act ends on the emotional turn you named: this isn't cynicism, it's relief — the org is weather, the ocean, the mountain. You can't fight it, and you don't have to be sad at it.
Final image: standing at the shore.

**Act 2 — Reading the Terrain (Lecture 2: How Power Actually Works)**
Dramatic question: where does power actually live, in this specific organization?
Now the structural lens becomes a tool. The formal org chart vs. the real one. Japan is the centerpiece because it names out loud what Western orgs hide: ringi confirms decisions rather than making them, nemawashi is where the decision happens, honne and tatemae are the two layers every org runs. The act teaches diagnostics — follow the budget, follow the information, follow who gets promoted, find the pre-meeting. Ends with the audience able to point at where a decision really happened.
Final image: the map.

**Act 3 — Moving Well (Lecture 3: Building and Exercising Power)**
Dramatic question: given all this, how do I act — and how do I get more power while doing it?
Navigation, not manipulation. Pfeffer's material — sponsorship, visibility, resource control, brokerage between groups — but framed as reading currents, the way a sailor does, which keeps it non-negative. The Stoic operating stance: Marcus's morning preparation, the dichotomy of control, "the impediment becomes the way." Ends by returning to the Annie quote one final time, but now the audience stands on the other side of it — she wasn't the sad one, she was the free one.
Final image: the sailor.

The emotional arc across the whole series: victim of the machine → reader of the machine → navigator of it. Naive moralism → calm clarity → calm agency. And each lecture repeats the three-act shape internally, so it's fractal — setup, turn, resolution in every hour too.

One structural note: the Annie quote opens Act 1 and closes Act 3, and the ocean/mountain image threads all three. That's what makes it feel like one story instead of three talks.
