# Act 3 — Moving Well
## Lecture 3: Building and Exercising Power

Dramatic question: given all this, how do I act — and how do I get more power while doing it?
Final image: the sailor.
Runtime: 60 minutes, ~27 slides.

Internal three acts:
- Setup (0–12): the operating stance — how to hold all of this without going bitter.
- Turn (12–45): navigation tactics — the concrete moves that build power.
- Resolution (45–60): the choice of which fights to fight, and the coffin-maker from the other side.

---

## Beat outline

### Cold open — the sailor (5 min, slides 1–3)
1. Title slide: "Part 3: Moving Well."
2. Image: a sailor. Not fighting the ocean, not sad about the ocean — reading it, trimmed to it, going somewhere.
3. Thesis: Act 1 gave you sight, Act 2 gave you a one-page map. This hour is about the sailing. Navigation, not manipulation.

### Part 0 — Use the map (5 min, slides 4–5)
4. Take out the one-page map from Act 2. Pfeffer's process continues from where Act 2 stopped: "What are their points of view likely to be?… What are their power bases?… What are your bases of power and influence? What bases of influence can you develop…?" then "choose a course of action" ([Pfeffer, Managing with Power (1992), summary](https://lirsa.cnam.fr/medias/fichier/pfefferhtml__1262961623620.html)). Every move in this hour starts from a field on the page.
5. The map-to-move key:
   - Manage-closely names + trust names → who to build sponsorship with (Move 1)
   - Top units and their signals → where to make your work visible (Move 2)
   - "Others need from them" / "needs from others" → which dependencies to create or reduce (Move 3; Kotter: successful managers cope with dependence "by eliminating or avoiding unnecessary dependence and by establishing power over those others," [Kotter 1977 notes](https://www.davidmays.org/BN/KotWhat.html))
   - Brokers and seams → where to broker (Move 4)
   - Decision trace (where the pre-meeting happened, who drafted) → whom to pre-wire, and what document to volunteer to write (Move 5)
   - Advice names + Step 1 promotion signals → which hard assignment to ask for (Move 6)
   - "Can say no / pocket-veto" + keep-satisfied quadrant → which battles not to start, and whose concerns to answer first (Move 7)
   - Seams → where a structural change would help (Move 8)

### Part 1 — The operating stance (12 min, slides 6–10)
6. Marcus Aurelius 2.1 — the morning preparation: "Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial… we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids" ([Long translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15877)). Not bitterness. A plan for the day.
7. The dichotomy of control (Epictetus, Enchiridion 1): "There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power" — and beyond it he lists "body, property, reputation, office" ([Higginson translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45109)). The structure — even your office — is not yours; your conduct is. Invest only where you have agency.
8. Not merely bearing the org, but using it. Meditations 5.20: "the mind converts and changes every hindrance to its activity into an aid; and so that which is a hindrance is made a furtherance to an act; and that which is an obstacle on the road helps us on this road" ([Long translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15877)). What stands in the way becomes the way. [Corrected: removed "amor fati" — Nietzsche's term, not Marcus's; replaced paraphrase with Long's text.]
9. Why this stance is the precondition for power: Pfeffer lists "the ability to tolerate conflict… resilience" among the "qualities that produce power" ([Pfeffer, Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/jeffrey-pfeffer-how-lean-power)). Calm is not a personality; it's a power skill. The emotional payoff restated: you said it yourself — this changed how you enjoy your job. You're not sad anymore because you're dealing with something like the ocean or the mountain.
10. Bridge: stance without tactics is just therapy. Now the moves.

### Part 2 — Navigation: how power is actually built (28 min, slides 11–22)
11. Pfeffer's frame: power is a skill set, not a personality trait — "political skill, like virtually all skills, can be improved through practice and coaching" ([Pfeffer, Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/jeffrey-pfeffer-how-lean-power)). [Removed unsourced "deniable if you're good at it."]
12. Move 1 — sponsorship. (Map: manage-closely + trust names.)  A sponsor isn't a mentor: per Sylvia Ann Hewlett (Forget a Mentor, Find a Sponsor, 2013), a sponsor uses senior capital to advocate for you, including when you're not in the room, and you earn it by delivering value to the sponsor's goals ([Forbes interview](https://www.forbes.com/sites/danschawbel/2013/09/10/sylvia-ann-hewlett-find-a-sponsor-instead-of-a-mentor/)). Do visible work that makes your sponsor look smart.
13. The Japanese frame for sponsorship: Nakane's oyabun/kobun — "literally, of parent to child," with "appropriate reciprocal behavior… expected from the respective parties" ([summary](http://roylin1003.pbworks.com/w/page/52314606/05%20-%20Ethnic%20Families%20in%20America%20%28Elsevier%2C%201998%2C%201976%29)); "mutual but asymmetrical," like Doi's amae ([Nanzan](https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/6/article/511/pdf/download)). The Western euphemism is "mentor"; the honest word is patron. Naming it takes the ick out of it — and it runs both ways: the patron needs loyal reach as much as you need cover.
14. Move 2 — visibility. (Map: top units.)  Pfeffer's example: former students who took the "unsung" job of running summer-associate recruiting — a task that brings "enormous amounts of visibility" because "you get to meet everybody in the firm" ([Pfeffer, HBR IdeaCast 2010](https://hbr.org/podcast/2010/06/telling-the-truth-about-power)). Manage the narrative of your work without self-promotion theater — reports, summaries, the pre-meeting.
15. Move 3 — resource control. (Map: needs/needed-by columns.)  "Whenever you have discretionary control over resources important to others—things like money, equipment, space, and information—you can use them to build your power"; "Access to information or influential people can be even more valuable" ([Pfeffer, "Power Play," HBR 2010](https://jeffreypfeffer.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/HBR-JulAug2010-PowerPlay-10.pdf)). Hold at least one that nobody else holds.
16. Move 4 — brokerage. (Map: brokers and seams.)  Pfeffer: "Ascertain which people or groups might benefit from being connected — and connect them (the technical term for this is filling structural holes" — Burt) ([Pfeffer, Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/jeffrey-pfeffer-how-lean-power)). Brokers see the nemawashi coming. Nakane sharpens it: where lateral ties stay weak, brokers are scarce — and valuable.
17. Move 5 — do the pre-meeting. (Map: decision trace.)  Now you do nemawashi yourself: decisions are won one-on-one before the meeting. This is the Act 2 map turned into motion. Meyer's cautionary cases inverted: the Beam exec who kept trying to win the Tokyo meeting lost it every time; once he gave his input weeks earlier, he stopped losing. Timing is the skill.
18. The framer as role model: the kiansha. "The person who writes the ringi is the person who frames the question, and who gathers all the data… Their selection of what information to include and what not to include is crucial" ([Japan Intercultural Consulting](https://japanintercultural.com/free-resources/articles/how-do-the-japanese-put-in-their-two-cents-ringi/)). Volunteer to write the document.
19. Move 6 — assignment before title: (Map: advice names + promotion signals.)  in a Japanese "late promotion" firm, the gap that predicted who made manager appeared "in the first personnel transfer after the initial assignment" ([Uehara 2009](https://www.jil.go.jp/english/JLR/documents/2009/JLR23_uehara.pdf)). Ask for the pilot, the turnaround, the thing that's failing — the title follows the assignment. And tend cohort ties horizontally on purpose, because vertical structures keep them weak (Nakane). [Corrected: Abegglen attribution replaced with Uehara.]
20. Move 7 — pick battles the org can absorb. (Map: can-say-no + keep-satisfied.)  Not every wrong is yours to fight: the structure — even "office" — sits on Epictetus's list of things "beyond our power" (slide 7). Spend effort where you have agency.
21. Move 8 — the inverse Conway maneuver: (Map: seams.)  "evolving your team and organizational structure to promote your desired architecture" ([ThoughtWorks Technology Radar, July 2014](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/inverse-conway-maneuver)). Redraw the seams between teams and you change what the org is capable of producing. You're not manipulating people, you're changing the terrain. Only people with real standing get to redraw seams, which makes this both a power move and a measure of how much power you have.
22. What this looks like over 12 months: a concrete sequence — map the terrain, hold one resource, broker one cross-unit thing, earn one sponsor, win one pre-meeting, ask for one hard assignment.

### Part 3 — The choice (13 min, slides 23–26)
23. The objection, finally: "if it's all structure, why bother being good?" Because the informal org runs on trust, and trust is the one resource you can't buy.
24. Navigation vs. manipulation, drawn plainly: manipulation uses the map to surprise people; navigation uses the map to arrive where decisions happen and be useful there. One burns trust, the other compounds it. Nemawashi vs. the backroom deal — same mechanics, opposite intent: one shares information early, the other hoards it.
25. You can play the game and keep your face — Doi again: tatemae and honne are "symbiotic, mutually constitutive" ([Japan Times review of The Anatomy of Self](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/culture/2001/09/16/books/a-theory-in-need-of-updating/)); the tatemae is the part of you that makes the group livable. Your honne stays yours.
26. The coffin-maker, one final time — now from the other side: "It is not that the carriage-maker is benevolent and the coffin-maker is cruel" ([Hanfeizi ch. 17, warringstates.day translation](https://warringstates.day/library/hanfeizi/17-bei-nei)). In Act 1 that line told you to stop hunting villains. Now it tells you something useful: positions shape what people want, and you can choose and shape yours. Series arc: victim of the machine → reader of the machine → navigator of it. Final image: the sailor, trimmed to the current, going somewhere. You don't have to love the machine. You don't have to fix it. You have to know it and move well through it. That's enough. That's the whole series.

### Close (2 min, slide 27)
27. Homework for the whole series: name one decision you'll win by pre-meeting this quarter; ask for one hard assignment; tell someone in the room which one.
