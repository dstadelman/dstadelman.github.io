# Act 2 — Reading the Terrain
## Lecture 2: How Power Actually Works

Dramatic question: where does power actually live, in this specific organization?
Final image: the map.
Runtime: 60 minutes, ~30 slides.

Internal three acts:
- Setup (0–10): the org chart you're given vs. the org that exists.
- Turn (10–42): Japan names out loud what Western orgs hide — the two-layer machine.
- Resolution (42–60): the playbook — the audience leaves with a one-page map of their own org.

---

## Beat outline

### Cold open — two org charts (5 min, slides 1–3)
1. Title slide: "Part 2: Reading the Terrain."
2. Two slides side by side: the org chart from the intranet, and a hand-drawn second chart — who talks to whom, who eats lunch with whom, whose approval actually matters. Same company, two machines.
3. Thesis: every formal org has an informal org running underneath it. The informal one decides. This is not corruption — it's physics.

### Part 1 — The mirror: Japan says it out loud (22 min, slides 4–15)
4. Why Japan is the centerpiece: Japanese business vocabulary names the two layers explicitly. Western orgs run the same layers but call them "politics" and feel guilty about it.
5. Erin Meyer's key distinction ("Being the Boss in Brussels, Boston, and Beijing," HBR 2017): authority and decision-making are two separate dimensions. The US is "an egalitarian culture where decisions are made top-down"; Japan is a "very hierarchical culture that also follows an extreme consensual decision-making pattern" ([Meyer, HBR 2017](https://erinmeyer.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Being-the-Boss-HBR.pdf); [HBR podcast, 2017](https://hbr.org/podcast/2017/07/how-authority-and-decision-making-differ-across-cultures)). This breaks the audience's default model in one slide. Small "d" vs big "D" decisions (Meyer): top-down cultures make quick decisions that may be "changed as more information arises"; in consensual cultures "it takes a long time to negotiate group agreement. However, once a decision gets made, implementation is surprisingly quick" — a Decision with a capital D. "Either system can work well" ([Meyer, HBR 2017](https://erinmeyer.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Being-the-Boss-HBR.pdf)). Both are rational adaptations; neither is "how decisions work."
6. The Suntory–Beam story (Meyer): a Beam manager flew to Japan with slides to sway the director — "During the meeting, it became apparent that the decision had already been made by the group beforehand and was different from my proposal. Trying to discuss and convince during the meeting had no effect at all." He "learned to give his input much earlier" ([Meyer, HBR 2017](https://erinmeyer.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Being-the-Boss-HBR.pdf)). Japanese pharma executives say the same from the other side: "foreigners try to make decisions and action plans during the meeting… but we cannot" ([EU-Japan Centre, Glisby & Holden 2018](https://www.eu-japan.eu/sites/default/files/report_from_understanding_to_navigating_japanese_business_culture.pdf)). The meeting was the receipt. This is the emotional core of the lecture — watch someone discover they were reading the terrain wrong. [Corrected: Astellas is not in Meyer; the "100% aligned" detail was unsourced and is removed.]
7. Ringi-seido: the document with everyone's hanko (seal) climbing the hierarchy to the president. Looks like the decision process. It isn't. Recent surveys "still support comprehensive American surveys dating back to the 1970s showing that up to 90% of all large Japanese companies follow the ringi decision-making system"; at Astellas the ringi-sho is "an on-line document supported by a dedicated software programme" ([EU-Japan Centre, Glisby & Holden 2018](https://www.eu-japan.eu/sites/default/files/report_from_understanding_to_navigating_japanese_business_culture.pdf)) — not a museum piece.
8. The killer line from the research: "One might say that the actual ringi is less about finding a decision than about confirming it." And: the informal process "is of much higher importance for the decision making than the actual ringi, since it is during the nemawashi process that the consent of every participant is mustered" ([Pudelko, "Decision making in Japanese companies"](https://uni-tuebingen.de/fileadmin/Uni_Tuebingen/Fakultaeten/WiSo/Wiwi/Uploads/Lehrstuehle/International_Management/Publications/Artikel_-_Decision_making_in_Japanese_companies.pdf)). [Corrected: the "Nakamura" quote could not be found; replaced with Pudelko.]
9. Nemawashi — literally "to 'dig around the roots'" ([EU-Japan Centre, Glisby & Holden 2018](https://www.eu-japan.eu/sites/default/files/report_from_understanding_to_navigating_japanese_business_culture.pdf)): the preparation before transplanting, so the tree survives the move. A group of "perhaps three younger employees… (the kiansha or plan initiators)" collects the opinions of everyone affected — "up to 80 people" — before the formal ringisho exists; "If a submitted proposal is rejected during this process, it has to be replaced by another and the process starts again" ([Pudelko, "Decision making in Japanese companies"](https://uni-tuebingen.de/fileadmin/Uni_Tuebingen/Fakultaeten/WiSo/Wiwi/Uploads/Lehrstuehle/International_Management/Publications/Artikel_-_Decision_making_in_Japanese_companies.pdf)). The meeting is the receipt.
10. Nemawashi is not lobbying ([EU-Japan Centre, Glisby & Holden 2018](https://www.eu-japan.eu/sites/default/files/report_from_understanding_to_navigating_japanese_business_culture.pdf)): it "does have a superficial similarity to lobbying" but "is an entirely different process." It "is respected as a positive and constructive activity, which: facilitates all-important information flows; discourages disharmony; eliminates unwanted surprises; avoids potential loss of face," and ensures "tacit knowledge is shared." Constructive maintenance, not dirty work.
11. The ringi's quiet veto: disagree? "simply put it in their desk drawer and never take it out again" — a pocket veto — or stamp "their hanko upside down." "Either of these is a far cry from having to say in front of other people in a meeting 'I disagree!'" ([Japan Intercultural Consulting](https://japanintercultural.com/free-resources/articles/how-do-the-japanese-put-in-their-two-cents-ringi/)). Conflict is designed out of the instrument.
12. Honne and tatemae via Takeo Doi (The Anatomy of Self, 1986; Japanese original Omote to Ura, 1985): NOT a false mask over a true self. As the Japan Times review summarizes Doi, the pair is "not to be read… as one 'versus' another… they are symbiotic, mutually constitutive… Without omote there could be no ura, without tatemae, no honne" ([Japan Times review](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/culture/2001/09/16/books/a-theory-in-need-of-updating/)). Both are real. This is the anti-cynicism slide: reading the two layers is not exposing a lie, it's reading a second truth.
13. Chie Nakane (Japanese Society, 1970): the vertical society. Frame beats attribute: Nakane's point is that people identify first by the company they belong to, not their profession, and that groups are built from one-to-one vertical ties while lateral ties stay weak. Hierarchy as weather, not injustice. [Removed unsourced "cohort defines superiors for life" and "triangle without a base."]
14. Late promotion is not anti-merit: Japanese firms use "a late promotion pattern," yet in one trading company (2,933 graduates hired 1962–1977) the disparity that predicted who reached manager showed up "in the first personnel transfer after the initial assignment" — "employees fight in a multilayered tournament shortly after joining" ([Uehara 2009, Japan Labor Review](https://www.jil.go.jp/english/JLR/documents/2009/JLR23_uehara.pdf)). The informal record (who got the hard assignment) runs ahead of the formal one (the title). That gap is exactly what the diagnostics measure. [Corrected: Abegglen & Stalk claim not verified; replaced with Uehara.]
15. The ringi document as a Conway object: its routing path — whose seal, in what order — is a literal, physical record of the communication structure. Same law as Conway's, two vocabularies. Ties the Japan half to the diagnostics half. Synthesis: the formal chart is the tatemae of the org. The informal network is its honne. Neither is fake.

### Part 2 — The playbook: map your org this month (25 min, slides 16–28)
Everything here is something a mid-level employee can do this week with no special access: look at what's already visible, ask questions in conversations you already have, write it down on one page.

16. Step 0 — Find the org's few critical issues. Not your issue: the two to four things leadership actually cares about. Look at the strategy doc or OKRs, the recurring themes of all-hands (and earnings calls, if public), where new budget and headcount went this year, and what keeps getting escalated. Write them across the top of the page. Pfeffer's diagnostic process (Managing with Power, 1992, ch. 3) starts the same way: decide what has to get done, then work out who is influential and important to getting it. Power runs through these issues: subunits that cope with the organization's critical uncertainties gain power (strategic contingencies theory, [Hickson, Hinings, Lee, Schneck & Pennings, "A Strategic Contingencies' Theory of Intraorganizational Power," Administrative Science Quarterly 16(2), 1971, pp. 216–229](https://doi.org/10.2307/2391831)). Every later step is done per critical issue; for the first pass, pick one as a practice run. This is a standing map, kept up over months, not a one-off.
17. Step 1 — Score the units from signals you can already see. Pfeffer (Managing with Power, 1992, ch. 3) scores units by observable indicators: representation in top management, recent headcount growth, reporting level, seats on cross-unit task forces, promotion rates, reputation, and budget. Where to look: leadership announcements, org-chart history, who staffs the task forces, who got promoted this year and what they had been assigned beforehand (Uehara: the assignment predicts the title, slide 14). Pfeffer: "budget is a source of power" ([Pfeffer, HBR IdeaCast 2010](https://hbr.org/podcast/2010/06/telling-the-truth-about-power)). Write down: the top 2–3 units for your issue. (Moved from Act 1's X-ray slides: if the company is public, its annual report's reportable segments show how it splits itself.)
18. Step 2 — Ask the network questions in conversations you already have. Krackhardt & Hanson map three networks: advice ("on whom others depend to solve problems"), trust ("which employees share delicate political information and back one another in a crisis") and communication. Their questions: "Whom do you talk to every day?" "Whom do you go to for help or advice at least once a week?" "Whom would you recruit to support a proposal of yours that could be unpopular?" "Whom would you trust to keep in confidence your concerns about a work-related issue?" Their rules: earn trust first, and "discount any answers not confirmed by both" people ([Krackhardt & Hanson, "Informal Networks: The Company Behind the Chart," HBR July–Aug 1993](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/krack/documents/pubs/1993/1993%20Informal%20Networks.pdf)). They designed these as a confidential company survey; a mid-level employee can ask the advice and talk-to questions informally in 1:1s and coffee chats. Write down: names that come up repeatedly, and whether each is an advice name, a trust name, or both.
19. Step 3 — Trace one recent decision backward. Pick a decision that was announced in a meeting and ask the people involved: when did you first hear about this, from whom, and who saw the proposal before the meeting? Meyer's Beam manager learned the decision "had already been made by the group beforehand" ([Meyer, HBR 2017](https://erinmeyer.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Being-the-Boss-HBR.pdf)); the person who drafts the proposal "frames the question" ([Japan Intercultural Consulting](https://japanintercultural.com/free-resources/articles/how-do-the-japanese-put-in-their-two-cents-ringi/)); and objections often kill proposals silently — the desk-drawer veto (slide 11). Write down: where the pre-meeting happened, who drafted, who saw it first, and anyone whose silence stalled it.
20. Step 4 — Dig with the access you already have. Only look at what your normal access already shows; don't request access in order to map people, and keep the page private. What to look at → what it reveals → what to write:
   - Systems: service-catalog entries, on-call rotations, runbooks, admin/owner fields → who actually keeps things running and can unblock you → system → maintainer.
   - Source code: a CODEOWNERS file lets a repo "define individuals or teams that are responsible for code," and "Code owners are automatically requested for review when someone opens a pull request that modifies code that they own" ([GitHub Docs, About code owners](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-code-owners)). `git blame` is documented to "Show what revision and author last modified each line of a file" ([git-blame docs](https://git-scm.com/docs/git-blame)); `git log` and PR history show who commits, reviews, and approves → the real technical owners → code area → approver. Conway: organizations "are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations" ([Conway 1968](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/committees.pdf)), so the code map is a communication map.
   - Meetings: who owns the recurring invites, runs staff meetings and steering committees, sets the agenda; who's required vs. optional → where agendas get set (controlling which issues get raised at all is what Bachrach & Baratz, "Two Faces of Power," 1962, call the second face of power) → forum → agenda owner → required attendees.
   - Shared calendars, distribution-list and Slack-channel owners, wiki/doc owners and edit history → who curates information → channel/doc → owner.
   - Approval workflows in ticketing, procurement and expense tools; escalation paths in runbooks and queues → who can say yes or no → request type → approver → escalation.
21. Step 5 — Map dependence and gatekeepers. Kotter: managers depend on "superiors, subordinates, peers in other parts of the organization, the subordinates of peers, outside suppliers, customers" and more, because of "division of labor and limited resources" ([Kotter, "Power, Dependence, and Effective Management," HBR July–Aug 1977](https://na.eventscloud.com/file_uploads/ad696b8ed37d8440b6d5518a0eb6aad3_PowerDependenceandEffectiveManagement.PDF)). For each name on your page, write what they need from others and what others need from them. Then note who controls access: "staff members who control senior executive calendars have an enormous amount of power" ([Pfeffer, HBR IdeaCast 2010](https://hbr.org/podcast/2010/06/telling-the-truth-about-power)) — note whose assistant or chief of staff decides what reaches the calendar.
22. Step 6 — Find the brokers and the seams. Brokers fill "structural holes" — they connect groups that would benefit from being connected ([Pfeffer, Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/jeffrey-pfeffer-how-lean-power); Burt 1992). Look for the people named in Step 2 by two groups that rarely talk to each other. Then follow the artifact (Conway, Act 1): which integrations, handoffs or reconciliations sit between teams. Write down: 1–3 brokers and 1–3 seams.
23. Step 7 — Plot everyone on a power/interest grid (Mendelow 1991, as taught by [Open University OpenLearn](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/science-maths-technology/computing-ict/successful-it-systems/content-section-4.4)). Two questions per person: how much can they affect the outcome (power, vertical axis), and how much do they care about this issue (interest, horizontal axis)?
   ```
   POWER
   high | KEEP SATISFIED      | MANAGE CLOSELY
        | brief them, don't   | involve early,
        | overload them       | pre-wire, co-own
        |---------------------+-----------------
        | MONITOR             | KEEP INFORMED
   low  | light touch         | regular updates;
        |                     | often your allies
        +---------------------------------------
          low            INTEREST           high
   ```
   Interest shifts as issues change, so recheck monthly. Write down: each name's quadrant.
24. The one-page map (fill in, keep private):
   - Critical issues (2–4) and the evidence each is critical; practice issue (Step 0)
   - Top units and the signals behind each (Step 1)
   - People table — name · formal role · advice name? · trust name? · can say no / pocket-veto? · needs from others · others need from them · gatekeeper to their calendar · power/interest quadrant (Steps 2, 5, 7)
   - Decision trace — where the pre-meeting happened · who drafted · who saw it first · who stalled it (Step 3)
   - Access dig: system → maintainer · code area → approver · forum → agenda owner → required attendees · channel/doc → owner · request type → approver → escalation (Step 4)
   - Brokers and seams (Step 6)
   - Date, and what to recheck next month (Step 7)
25. Worked example (FICTIONAL: invented company and people, so no sources needed). Larkspur Analytics, a made-up 400-person B2B SaaS company. Priya is a senior engineer on the Integrations team. Step 0: the OKRs, the last three all-hands and this year's headcount point to three critical issues: (A) closing enterprise deals, which stall on security reviews; (B) billing accuracy, since bad invoices hit month-end close; (C) cutting cloud cost. Practice issue: replacing the flaky nightly billing export, which touches B and C. Step 1: Platform grew from 30 to 45 people last year, its director sits on exec staff, and the last two staff-engineer promotions came from Platform; Integrations shrank. Step 2: asked in 1:1s "Whom do you go to for help or advice at least once a week?", four of six engineers named Marco (Platform staff engineer), not their managers; asked "Whom would you trust to keep in confidence your concerns about a work-related issue?", two named Dana (Finance systems analyst). Marco and Dana each confirmed. Step 3: the last export decision was "made" at Platform Steering, but Marco first saw it in a pre-read doc Lena (Platform director) circulated a week earlier; Finance's objection never reached the agenda.
26. Fictional case, Step 4, the dig. CODEOWNERS excerpt: `/billing/export/  @larkspur/platform-core` and `/billing/ledger/  @dana-fin`. `git log` on `/billing/export/` for the past year: 60% of commits from Integrations, every merge approved by Marco. On-call rotation for billing-export: Integrations only, so Priya's team carries the pager for code another team approves. Platform Steering invite: owner Lena; required = Platform leads + VP Eng; Finance optional. Procurement workflow: infra spend over $10k → Lena → VP Eng; Finance tooling spend → Controller.
27. Fictional case, Steps 5–7. Dependence: Priya needs Marco's approvals and Lena's agenda slot; Lena needs fewer Sev-2 billing incidents (her steering deck reports them); Dana needs clean export data for month-end close. Broker: Dana, the only person named by both Finance and Engineering. Seam: the export, written by one team, approved by a second, consumed by a third, just as Conway predicts. Grid: Lena, VP Eng = manage closely; Controller = keep satisfied; Marco, Dana = keep informed; Integrations peers = monitor. Full map: appendix.
28. Handoff: the map is not for manipulation. It's for navigation — you can't sail a current you can't see. Less surprise, less offense, fewer ambushes. Next hour: what to do with each field on this page.
