# Act 2 — Reading the Terrain
## Lecture 2: How Power Actually Works

Dramatic question: where does power actually live, in this specific organization?
Final image: the map.
Runtime: 60 minutes, ~29 slides.

Internal three acts:
- Setup (0–10): the org chart you're given vs. the org that exists.
- Turn (10–42): Japan names out loud what Western orgs hide — the two-layer machine.
- Resolution (42–60): diagnostics — the audience can now point at where a decision really happened.

---

## Beat outline

### Cold open — two org charts (5 min, slides 1–3)
1. Title slide: "Part 2: Reading the Terrain."
2. Two slides side by side: the org chart from the intranet, and a hand-drawn second chart — who talks to whom, who eats lunch with whom, whose approval actually matters. Same company, two machines.
3. Thesis: every formal org has an informal org running underneath it. The informal one decides. This is not corruption — it's physics.

### Part 1 — The mirror: Japan says it out loud (22 min, slides 4–15)
4. Why Japan is the centerpiece: Japanese business vocabulary names the two layers explicitly. Western orgs run the same layers but call them "politics" and feel guilty about it.
5. Erin Meyer's key distinction (Culture Map / HBR "Culture Map" work): authority and decision-making are two separate dimensions, and cultures don't correlate them the way we assume. The US is egalitarian but top-down in deciding; Japan is strongly hierarchical AND the most consensual in deciding. This breaks the audience's default model in one slide.
6. Small "d" vs capital "D" decisions (Meyer): American decisions are made fast and revised constantly — lowercase d, implementation drags. Japanese decisions take months but once made are fixed and implementation is swift. Both are rational adaptations; neither is "how decisions work."
7. The Astellas/Beam stories (Meyer): Western execs fly to Tokyo believing the meeting is where the decision happens, present their slides, and find the group 100% aligned the other way. The meeting was the receipt. This is the emotional core of the lecture — watch someone discover they were reading the terrain wrong.
8. Ringi-seido: the document with everyone's hanko (seal) climbing the hierarchy to the president. Looks like the decision process. It isn't. Surveys still show large majorities of big Japanese firms using it; at Astellas it runs on dedicated software — not a museum piece.
9. The killer line from the research: "the actual ringi is less about finding a decision than about confirming it." And: "the essential decision is often made by the nemawashi process and the ringi is the formal procedure of writing and detailing the decision."
10. Nemawashi — literally gardening: digging around the roots before transplanting, so the tree survives the move. The kiansha (young plan initiators) quietly canvass every affected party one-on-one — sometimes 80 people — before a document exists. Rejected during nemawashi? Start over. The meeting is the receipt.
11. Nemawashi is not lobbying (EU-Japan business guide): lobbying implies persuasion after positions form; nemawashi prevents positions from forming — it shares tacit knowledge, eliminates surprises, avoids loss of face. Respected as constructive maintenance, not dirty work.
12. The ringi's quiet veto: disagree? Put it in your desk drawer (a pocket veto), or stamp your seal upside down. No one ever has to say "I disagree" in front of the group. Conflict is designed out of the instrument.
13. Honne and tatemae via Takeo Doi (The Anatomy of Self): NOT a false mask over a true self. Doi's point — they are symbiotic, mutually constitutive; without tatemae there is no honne. Both are real; social reality has more than one kind of truth. This is the anti-cynicism slide: reading the two layers is not exposing a lie, it's reading a second truth.
14. Senpai / kohai / doki via Chie Nakane (Japanese Society): the vertical society. Frame (ba) beats attribute (shikaku) — a Japanese introduces themself by company, not job. Cohort of entry defines superiors and inferiors for life. The group is a triangle without a base: strong vertical links, weak horizontal ones. Age and cohort as weather, not injustice.
15. Abegglen & Stalk (Kaisha) + Nakane: the seniority system is not anti-merit — entry cohorts all start identical, the able get harder assignments years before the title catches up, and the long game selects for ability. The informal record (who got the hard assignment) runs ahead of the formal one (the title). That gap is exactly what the diagnostics measure.
16. Synthesis: the formal chart is the tatemae of the org. The informal network is its honne. Neither is fake.
17. The ringi document as a Conway object: its routing path — whose seal, in what order — is a literal, physical record of the communication structure. Same law as Conway's, two vocabularies. Ties the Japan half to the diagnostics half.

### Part 2 — Diagnostics: how to map your own terrain (20 min, slides 18–26)
18. Diagnostic 1 — follow the budget. Who signs, who can stop, who reallocates without asking. Budget authority is the only org chart that never lies.
19. Diagnostic 2 — follow the information. Who gets the deck before the meeting. Who's on the distribution list. Who's CC'd late.
20. Diagnostic 3 — follow the promotions. Who gets promoted and fast is the org's revealed religion. (Annie's method, applied.) The Abegglen lesson: watch the assignment, not the title — the informal record runs ahead.
21. Diagnostic 4 — find the pre-meeting. Every important meeting has a nemawashi phase. Find where it happens: the 1:1, the group chat, the hallway.
22. Diagnostic 5 — meeting mechanics: who speaks first, who speaks last, who summarizes, who nobody contradicts. The last speaker usually owns the meeting. (And per Meyer: if your org decides with capital D, the meeting is the receipt — stop trying to win meetings.)
23. Diagnostic 6 — the calendar. Access to time is access to power. Who can get 30 minutes, and how fast.
24. Diagnostic 7 — the brokers. The people who connect groups that don't normally talk. Structural holes (Burt) — power lives in the gaps between clusters. Nakane's warning: vertical societies make horizontal ties weak — brokers are rarer and more powerful there.
25. Diagnostic 8 — follow the artifact (Conway's Law as a tool). The shape of what the company actually ships is the honne: product boundaries, where the interfaces are, which integrations are held together by one person, which teams' work has to be manually reconciled. The most empirical diagnostic — no access needed, no interviews, nobody can lie to you about it.
26. Worked example: trace one real decision end-to-end using the eight diagnostics, starting from the artifact and working back to the people. (Anonymized or historical — e.g., a well-documented public decision.)

### Part 3 — What the map is for (8 min, slides 27–28)
27. The map is not for manipulation. It's for navigation — you can't sail a current you can't see. What reading terrain actually feels like: less surprise, less offense, fewer ambushes. You show up where the decision is being made, not where the calendar says it is.
28. Homework from Act 1 debrief: where did your decision actually happen?

### Close (3 min, slide 29)
29. Final image: the map. Next time — given the terrain, how do you move? Act 3: Moving Well.
