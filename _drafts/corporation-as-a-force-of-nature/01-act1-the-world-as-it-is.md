### Part 1 — The corporation was never designed (15 min, slides 4–10)
4. English East India Company 1600, Dutch VOC 1602 — corporations as charters, not blueprints. They are usually credited with the "textbook" features: permanent capital, legal personhood, separation of ownership and management, limited liability, tradable shares — but the VOC got them one by one, and the EIC only in the 1650s (Gelderblom, De Jong & Jonker 2013).
5. The patchwork history (Gelderblom, De Jong, Jonker): permanent capital arrived 1612, director limited liability 1623 — each feature patched in to fix a crisis — "piecemeal engineering to remedy design flaws," driven by "severe cash constraints resulting from its military and commercial operations." Transferable shares and shareholder limited liability existed from the start; "the five features did not come as a package." "Legal form followed economic function, not the other way around." (Gelderblom, De Jong & Jonker, JEH 2013, [paper](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/server/api/core/bitstreams/40233814-d2c9-4c5d-bf08-995db6f11931/content))
6. Point slide: structures evolve under pressure — usually pressure about capital and control, never about human goodness.
7. Alfred Chandler, The Visible Hand: salaried managers replace owners; separation of ownership and control. TODO: pull a verified page-cited Chandler quote (not checked online).
8. The M-form (multi-divisional structure) — General Motors, DuPont, Sloan. Divisions as internal nations. TODO: source is Chandler, Strategy and Structure (1962), not The Visible Hand alone — add to sources and verify dates.
9. The ownership/control gap is where corporate politics lives — nobody runs the machine for the reasons the machine claims.

### Part 2 — Why hierarchy always re-forms (15 min, slides 11–18)
11. Robert Michels 1911, Political Parties: "Who says organization, says oligarchy." ([Michels, Political Parties, McMaster text](https://socserv.socsci.mcmaster.ca/~econ/ugcm/3ll3/michels/polipart.pdf)) Michels studied the German SPD — a party founded on democracy and equality.
12. The iron law: delegation creates specialists, specialists entrench. Even orgs founded to prevent hierarchy become hierarchical.
13. Max Weber: the iron cage of bureaucracy — rule by files, by rules, by position.
14. Han Feizi, two thousand years earlier, from the Chinese side: don't trust the minister's heart, read the position he stands in. The oldest structuralist in the room.
15. Conway's Law (Melvin Conway, 1968, Datamation): "organizations which design systems ... are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations." His own example: eight people, five on COBOL and three on ALGOL — the COBOL compiler ran in five phases, the ALGOL in three. "Two military services ... produced a copy of their organization chart." ([Conway, How Do Committees Invent?](https://www.melconway.com/Home/Committees_Paper.html)) The Annie thesis in engineering terms — structure doesn't just reveal what people are, it determines what they can produce.
16. Conway is testable in a way Michels never was. Nagappan, Murphy & Basili (Microsoft Research, 2008): on Windows Vista, organizational-structure metrics predicted failure-prone binaries better than churn, complexity or coverage ([TR-2008-11](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr-2008-11.pdf)). Markovtsev et al. (2019) restored GitLab's engineering org "in broad strokes" from commit activity alone across 117 projects ([arXiv 1905.06782](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06782)). The thing you shipped is a fossil record of the org. [Corrected: "Vaish, Gajos & Manku (2017)" could not be found; replaced with these two verified studies.]
17. Synthesis slide: four traditions, one finding — hierarchy re-forms because that is what the structure selects for.
18. The Annie line, restated academically: the structure is the evidence of human nature.

### Part 3 — Structure as an X-ray of values (12 min, slides 19–24)
19. How to read what an org actually believes: what gets measured, what gets promoted, how units are split, where budget authority sits.
20. Example: a company split into competing reportable segments believes internal competition produces accountability.
21. Example: a company that rotates execs through units believes loyalty is to the system, not the unit.
22. The human-nature answer to why we stay: Takeo Doi's amae (甘え) — "to depend and presume upon another's benevolence," the child's trust that the caregiver will indulge. Doi's claim: Japan is the only culture with an extensive vocabulary for it (amaeru, suneru to pout, tereru to be ashamed of the wish, hinekureru to take devious ways to deny it), which means the feeling is universal but only one culture institutionalized it. The corporation is a modern answer to amae: it offers security and belonging, and asks for loyalty in return.
23. Ruth Benedict's U-curve, quoted by Doi: the arc of life in Japan is the reverse of the American one — maximum indulgence for babies and the old, maximum restriction in the middle. The org sits in that restricted middle. Naming it takes the resentment out of it.
24. Exercise prompt: your org's X-ray — three questions to ask this week.

### Part 4 — The emotional turn (10 min, slides 25–28)
25. The objection: isn't this bleak? Isn't this giving up?
26. The turn: it isn't cynicism, it's relief. Nothing feels like betrayal once you read structure. A wave isn't malicious. A landslide isn't personal. You stop being sad at the mountain for being a mountain.
27. Marcus Aurelius 4.3 (Hays translation wording; TODO confirm exact phrasing against the edition you quote) — nowhere a quieter retreat than in one's own mind. The calm isn't escaping to the mountain; it's the disposition you bring to the meeting room. And 5.20: the impediment to action advances action. You can't fight the organization, and you don't have to be sad about it. You just gotta go.
28. Bridge to Act 2: next time, we learn to read the specific terrain — and we'll borrow a culture that names out loud what ours hides.
