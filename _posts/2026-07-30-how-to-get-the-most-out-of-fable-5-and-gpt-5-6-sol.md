The AI Daily Brief's latest episode is a deep-dive into prompting strategies for two frontier models released during a turbulent period in the industry -- Claude Fable and GPT-5.6 Sol. Rather than covering the latest model release drama, the host curates expert guidance from OpenAI's own team, UX researchers at Intuit, and veteran prompt engineers to show how effective use of these models requires fundamentally different interaction patterns than what got results with their predecessors.

* **Boundaries matter more with powerful models:** An official OpenAI Codex team guide warns that GPT-5.6 Sol's "tenacity" means the model will assert agency beyond what users intend. Setting explicit limits -- keep approved dates unchanged, use only supplied sources, flag missing info instead of guessing -- is critical not just for cost but to prevent real-world consequences like sending unapproved drafts.
* **Iterative steering cuts latency:** OpenAI now allows you to send follow-up messages while the model is still working, dynamically changing direction or adding detail mid-execution. This shifts interaction from turn-based prompting to continuous collaboration -- a pattern that becomes increasingly important as models get more powerful. Official guidance separates best practices for ChatGPT (Chat vs Work modes), with "Work" tasks optimized for recurring time-consuming jobs where one result can be reviewed before extending.
* **Think in tiers, not max:** OpenAI's developer site introduces GPT-5.6's compute dials: Sol for hard problems, Terra for business work, Luna for cheap fast tasks, each with six effort levels from none to max. The official advice -- start at your last model's setting, then test one level lower -- may feel counterintuitive but saves tokens and often produces cleaner output. New models need less prompting scaffolding: removing redundant instruction rules raised scores by 10-15% while cutting tokens up to 66%.
* **Concrete behavior beats abstract tone:** OpenAI's guidance on GPT-5.6 says "friendly" or "empathetic" are too vague. Instead, specify actual writing behaviors -- "name the customer's problem in your first line, give the fix as numbered steps, skip the apology paragraph" -- and you get consistent results every time.
* **Ambition is the real unlock:** Christine Zhu, an AI UX PM at Intuit, argues users don't push frontier models far enough -- they confine them to "optics" (making progress visible) and "execution" (automating busywork) while hoarding "impact" work for themselves. She maps three tiers of ambition: automating production updates ruthlessly, using AI as a coach that notices your personal patterns, and deploying it as a strategic sparring partner for hard decisions you'd normally avoid.
* **Loop-based interaction is the new pattern:** Multiple sources converge on putting models in self-critical loops -- build, check against a bar, close gaps, repeat. Three loop types are distinguished: turn-based (user directs each turn), goal-based (evaluator checks exit criteria), and proactive/time-based (scheduled, recurring). The key insight: Fable never decides it's finished; the human sets the stopping condition.
* **Unknowns are the real bottleneck:** A member of Claude Code's team argues that with agents like Fable 5, quality is bottlenecked not by your prompt instructions but by your ability to clarify what you haven't thought about -- organizing unknowns into four categories (known knowns, known unknowns, unknown knowns, unknown unknowns) and using the agent as a co-discoverer of blind spots.
* **New model release = context hygiene opportunity:** Prompt engineer Daniel Miessler recommends rerunning "meta-prompts" when any significant intelligence jump lands -- auditing your harness for stale beliefs about yourself, testing the model at maximum scope, and reorienting priorities with deep analysis across all your active projects.

The episode's overarching thesis: treating AI just as a faster version of what you already do is a missed opportunity. The real unlock happens when you combine new interaction patterns (loops, iterative steering) with ambition -- asking harder questions, pushing models into impact-tier work, and accepting that the hardest part isn't prompting but figuring out what to ask differently now that these tools can actually answer.
