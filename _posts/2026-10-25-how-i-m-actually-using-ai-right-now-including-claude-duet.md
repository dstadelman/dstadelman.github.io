# Summary: How I'm Actually Using AI Right Now (Including Claude Duet)

[AI Daily Brief - How I'm Actually Using AI Right Now (Including Claude Duet)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-Im-Actually-Using-AI-Right-Now-Including-Claude-Duet-e3r21ss)

Anthropic opened a research preview of Claude Duet on October 21, 2026 -- a proactive personal agent joining Meta's Muse and OpenAI's dots in the fast-forming category that the open-source OpenClaw wave kicked off earlier this year. Host Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW) uses his few days with Duet as a pretext for a full tour of his actual AI stack: which models he uses for strategy, research, copywriting, image generation, data analysis, and building -- and an honest verdict on whether personal agents are chiefs of staff or just proactive proxy-yous. His conclusion so far: much less a new way of interacting with AI, much more an easy-to-set-up little baby version of himself that does the stuff he doesn't want to do.

* **Personal agents are still an experimental form factor**: there is real traction -- Muse passed 10 million US downloads in 40 days and Meta claims 25% of its users are daily -- but the unresolved question is whether these agents organize everything you do like a chief of staff, or act as a specific proactive proxy that figures out how to be helpful without being asked.
* **Strategic externalization is his #1 use case -- about 60% of all AI time**: NLW thinks by talking, with reams of Notion "walk notes" from walking, driving, and thinking sessions across the AI Daily Brief and his company Superintelligent. Every major new model gets tested against this use case for one-to-one replacement potential.
* **The hardest model test is pushback that's based on something**: for years models were barely better than writing on paper because every idea "shines like gold." He wants critical engagement grounded in actual reasoning, not a model trained that pushback is good -- dynamic, creative, adaptable, and confident is a really hard line. Opus 5.5 is his best strategy model in a long time, displacing Fable 5.1; GPT models have felt flat for this work most of the year.
* **For high-stakes research, Astra and Opus grade each other**: because research ends up said publicly on AIDB (and on Love Murder, the true crime podcast he produces for his wife), sourcing and hallucination-resistance are non-negotiable. On important topics he runs both models and has each review the other's output as an extra confidence layer.
* **The AI show uses shockingly little AI to get made**: NLW surveys the day's news and discourse each evening, a researcher in Australia -- a 24-hour operation hack -- builds an overnight dossier of sources and social commentary, and NLW lays out screens and lets it rip unscripted.
* **Copywriting stays with Astra because of the memory moat**: episode descriptions and social posts run through AI daily, and titles are a 50/50 back-and-forth where the AI gets a chance to argue him off his draft. Even if Opus or Fable were equally good, the long accumulated track record of likes and dislikes makes re-tuning not worth it -- plus OpenAI's models get credit for concision.
* **Image generation has consolidated on the GPT image models**: not because they're necessarily better, but because daily cover images follow right after locking the headline, so it all lives in one thread. Ideogram wins for iteration (four images at a time with variance toggles), Midjourney for when you want something visually arresting and can give the model latitude.
* **Data analysis means Astra inside Codex**: from podcast statistics to the tax-extension ritual of dumping in a year of financial data, it gives the best results plus a concision of thought that suits the work.
* **Building has landed on Claude Code with Opus 5.5, effort high or extra**: after cycling through OpenClaw, Codex, Claude Code, and Cursor, the organizing and orchestration all happen in Claude Code -- with Codex-plus-Astra reserved for builds needing native GPT image generation, like visual companion experiences.
* **Fable's real breakthrough was lowering "completion energy"**: starting things had been easy for a while, but projects hung at 80-90% done. Fable brought completion energy down to match activation energy -- NLW built the entire first draft of the AIDB website's transcript-to-shareable-assets pipeline the first afternoon he got access, the day before Fable was taken offline in the export-control dust-up with the White House.
* **The AIDB website is a production pipeline, not a website**: it ingests each episode's transcript and extracts it into shareable web assets with a no-image-generation aesthetic; a secondary pipeline generates tweets and a LinkedIn post scheduled through Typefully (buying the plumbing beat wiring platform APIs directly); and a video pipeline picks the most compelling self-contained 60-90 seconds from each transcript, extracts the clip, and packages visual assets -- Claude's video editing jumped enormously with Opus 5.5, worth experimenting with even if you think you don't need video.
* **New show: How We Got Here, weekly, launched Monday October 26**: a 45-minute-to-hour show tracing the social, cultural, technological, and economic decisions behind today's biggest stories. First episode: how America fell out of love with the tech industry. After recording, the raw transcript plus research goes back to Opus 5.5 for a 5,000-6,000-word companion essay draft, which gets styled in Codex with Astra and GPT image -- then NLW rewrites about half of it by hand.
* **Proactive agents failed the juice-versus-squeeze test -- until maybe now**: his OpenClaw-era experiments (individual claws on heartbeats and cron jobs, a chief-of-staff coordinator, a persistent research agent) all got shut down because maintenance outweighed value. Partly personal: 90% of his calls are unscheduled and his inbox system is starring things and clearing every couple of days. It's also why he never covered the Hermes agent despite thinking it's an excellent product -- it just hasn't fit how he works.
* **Explaining the flight to an agent takes as long as booking it**: the canonical consumer pitches -- flight booking, agentic shopping, restaurants -- don't fit someone who books within one airline's miles ecosystem. The professional side of personal agents was always the only live question for him.
* **The monothread paradigm is finally possible thanks to compaction**: instead of chats scattered across projects that each hold only their own context, one thread holds everything, pulls up what it needs, and spins up subagents on demand. This only became viable with back-end advances in context compression -- early-2026 education programs literally taught handoff documents as a workaround.
* **The ecosystem triage**: third-party agents like Instinct (which raised $1B at a $10B valuation in September) lose on his unwillingness to move work context out of ChatGPT and Claude; Muse skews too personal for now, though Alexandr Wang's ambition clearly doesn't end there; Grokbot earns a "stay just engaged enough not to start from zero" allocation for its X integration; Google isn't worth heavy investment yet; Microsoft is tracked professionally but skipped by a solo operator biased toward nimble tools.
* **OpenAI's dot is weak on proactivity and too flat for strategy**: his dot, Quasar, responded to a dumped six-hour strategy conversation with essentially "great, this information has been saved for the future." As a feature it adds little to ChatGPT's reactive strengths, but it may have value as a master holder of context you dump once instead of repeating across chats.
* **Duet is so biased toward proactivity it overvalues work it can be proactive about**: it connected Slack/Gmail/Drive immediately, then policed a podcast ad-slot "double-booking" that wasn't a problem -- reminding him four times before he killed the entire workstream. Being able to be proactive about something isn't the same as it mattering.
* **Where Duet earns its keep: the starred-email problem**: for exactly the kind of email he'd star and return to days later, Duet has proposed responses he can edit or authorize from chat -- a couple sent as-is, and the most substantive creative proposal got him 90% of the way there. He also likes the web interface: channels, Claude artifact integration, and automatic task grouping into active/up next/scheduled/resolved. A few days in, this is the most optimistic he's been about the form factor.
* **Don't sweat which personal-agent ecosystem you pick**: the moment a feature works in one product, it becomes part of all of them within weeks if not days -- if dots' lack of proactivity is a common complaint, OpenAI will tweak it; if people prefer Duet's UI, the other labs will copy it.

The through line: personal agents haven't become the main way NLW interacts with AI -- they're an easy-to-set-up little baby version of himself doing a bunch of stuff he doesn't want to do, which is valuable, but whether it's future-of-the-industry valuable remains to be seen.
