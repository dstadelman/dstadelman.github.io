# Summary: AI Won't Make Our Minds Useless According to 400 Years of History

[The AI Daily Brief - AI Won't Make Our Minds Useless According to 400 Years of History](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/AI-Wont-Make-Our-Minds-Useless-According-to-400-Years-of-History-e3rhmc6)

In this Long Read Sunday episode, host Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW) reads extended excerpts from Venkatesh Rao's widely circulated essay "Cognitive Mercantilism," published October 25, 2026 on Rao's Contraptions Substack. The essay, which grew out of Rao's reading of historian Joyce Appleby's *The Relentless Revolution: A History of Capitalism*, argues that today's AI panic repeats the zero-sum mercantilist thinking that capitalism overturned four centuries ago. NLW pairs it with his own earlier framework on demand elasticity to make the case that abundant intelligence may free minds rather than make them obsolete.

* **Cognitive mercantilism defined**: Rao argues that AI anxiety mirrors 17th-century mercantilism once you swap wealth for intelligence -- intelligence is imagined as a fixed, scarce resource to be hoarded like bullion through talent grabs, IP control, and a favorable balance of knowledge trade.
* **The four mercantilist beliefs, updated**: wealth is finite; ordinary people can't be trusted with freedom; economics is about controlling supply while demand barely registers; and most people are good for nothing but subsistence labor -- so automating their work supposedly creates "billions of useless minds."
* **The agricultural counter-example**: productivity gains shrank farming from employing a large majority to a tiny minority, and the result was not useless mouths but abundant food and entirely new economic lives -- Rao, who jokes he "can barely keep a houseplant alive," notes you don't need to produce food to enjoy it, and the same may hold for intelligence.
* **NLW's demand elasticity framework**: revisiting his May episode "The New Jobs AI Will Create," NLW argues the lump-of-labor fallacy hides a second error -- assuming demand is fixed -- and lays out six elasticities AI can unlock: price, access, complexity, continuity, personalization, and relational.
* **The consumer revolution parallel**: early modern Britons voluntarily worked more days to afford new goods like tea, sugar, and Indian cottons, creating a virtuous cycle of desire, labor, and productivity -- Rao calls the AI analogue a "cognitive industrious revolution," where people think and build harder because more ambitions become reachable.
* **Why AI makes us work more, not less**: Rao says the returns on attention rise when tools unlock real leverage ("many people who appear stupid are simply not motivated to think, given the poor returns"), while NLW adds his "infinite backlog" concept -- agents that replicate yourself remove the natural calibration that made "done" possible.
* **AI Malthusianism**: Rao distinguishes a second fear-grammar from mercantilism -- not losing control of scarce intelligence, but being drowned by abundant intelligence; both assume demand for intelligence is fundamentally limited, and both historically resemble doctrines that innovation proved wrong.
* **A new labor politics of cognitive abundance**: the enduring political demand may not be preserving cognitive jobs, any more than industrial labor sought to preserve farm jobs, but securing a fair share of expanding cognitive consumption -- access to intelligence, the ability to command it, and rights to participate in an intelligence-rich economy.
* **Mercantilism was not for merchants**: mercantilism served statesmen and rentier elites while actual merchants birthed capitalism; Rao argues AI-industry leaders behave like cognitive mercantilists in proportion to their political ambitions, experiencing open weights and global diffusion mainly as threats to status and leverage.
* **NLW's caveat**: none of this argues against AI safety concerns -- transitions between economic orders are never without cost, but managing the transition looks very different from trying to stop it.

The core takeaway: if demand for intelligence proves as elastic and inventive as demand for material goods turned out to be, the "surplus of useless minds" is an artifact of failing to imagine what people would do in an intelligence-rich world -- and the most important consequence of the AI revolution may not be cheaper intelligence production, but most minds, human and artificial, being freed from producing intelligence merely to survive.
