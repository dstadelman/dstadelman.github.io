The AI Daily Brief covers the emerging slash goal primitive for AI coding agents like OpenAI Codex and Claude Code, explaining how it fundamentally shifts how power users interact with AI assistants. The episode walks through what slash goals are, why they matter, and which knowledge work tasks benefit most from this new paradigm.

* **Slash goals are not bigger prompts -- they are a fundamentally different paradigm**: Traditional AI interaction is turn-based -- you prompt, wait, review, give feedback, repeat. Slash goals break this cycle by creating a continuous loop where you define the outcome, the system checks against defined evidence, and autonomously decides whether to continue, complete, or stop blocked. This was first popularized by OpenAI's Pavel Hurin: you state the outcome, the model loops, self-evaluates, and stops when done.
* **The evolution from hacks to product primitives**: Early autonomous patterns like the "Ralph Wiggum Loop" (an improvised self-looping prompt pattern), writing custom while loops in 2024, and prompt files in 2025 have now crystallized into slash goal as a first-class product feature in 2026. Microsoft's Nicholas Bustamante (@nicbstme) described this as a significant milestone -- the loop is no longer something you code yourself; it's a built-in primitive. OpenAI's @gregorz7 documented how to explain the concept to non-technical audiences, and Claude Code rapidly adopted the same naming convention.
* **Writing effective goals requires precision, not volume**: The strongest goals define six elements -- the outcome, verification surface (tests, citations, rubrics), constraints (what must not regress), boundaries (files/tools/resources), iteration policy (how to decide the next move), and block/stop condition. There is a "Goldilocks zone" for scope: too narrow (fix this one line) leaves no flexibility to discover the real issue; too broad (improve the whole system) makes verification impossible. Defining a strong output artifact -- like "produce a docs page with two examples where all commands match current CLI behavior" -- is often the difference between success and failure.
* **The real power for knowledge work lies in auditing**: For non-coding tasks, slash goals excel when the output is not just an answer but an audit trail -- a ledger of what was checked, corroborated, contradicted, and what remains uncertain. User-provided rubrics (hiring criteria, vendor scorecards, editorial standards) are likely the most common near-term use case because the user supplies the criteria and the slash goal executes an entire review process rather than a single-pass prompt.
* **Highly specific non-coding applications that benefit**: Claim audits (verify each claim against sources, produce a table with supported/contradicted/unverified ratings and citations), market landscapes (build comparison tables with confidence levels and evidence gaps rather than a generic research summary), literature reviews (highlight conflicting evidence and disputed findings rather than flattening complexity), along with vendor evaluations, due diligence, policy research, interview synthesis, timeline reconstruction, spreadsheet audits, and strategy memos where messy inputs need structured output.
* **Traditional prompts remain appropriate most of the time**: Slash goals are not a replacement -- for smaller outcomes or tasks where success criteria are hard to define cleanly, standard prompt-based interaction is still the right tool. The autonomous spectrum includes different methods for different work, and Jason Lin's Codex Maxing tips remain vital even in the slash goal era because full disconnection from the process is not always desirable. This is a tool to experiment with, likely via tasks outside your core work, to develop intuition for when it truly adds value.

The overarching takeaway is that slash goals represent a shift from workflow -- you stop telling AI what to do and start telling it what you want achieved when you're finished, while retaining full lifecycle control through pause, resume, and clear commands. The system stays anchored to evidence-based completion rather than your impressions, making it particularly powerful for any work that produces an auditable artifact rather than a simple answer.
