# Summary: How Business AI Use is Changing

[The AI Daily Brief - How Business AI Use is Changing](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-Business-AI-Use-is-Changing-e3sj17g)

Three fresh enterprise AI datasets -- Databricks' new AI Index, Arcana Research's first annual state-of-enterprise-AI study, and the November Ramp AI Index -- landed within days of each other and tell a convergent story. Business AI has become a buyer's market: token price competition is pushing prices down, enterprises are switching and mixing model providers far more readily, and leading companies are growing usage much faster than spend. The open question is whether the top 1% of AI spenders, at hundreds of times the median, are outliers or a preview of where everyone else is heading.

* **Usage growth is outpacing spend growth**: Ramp found token consumption across its businesses rose 36% in a month while spend rose just 2%, because the average price per million tokens across OpenAI and Anthropic fell 21% in October -- pressure driven not just by cheaper Chinese models but by their mere presence forcing the big providers to cut prices.
* **Single-provider enterprises went from majority to minority in four months**: In July, 64% of companies on Databricks' Unity Gateway used only one model provider; by early November, 72% used two or more. Databricks argues raw token price is the wrong metric and that enterprises are increasingly focused on cost per successful task.
* **AI is an eight-figure budget line, and most leaders can't see it**: Arcana's Global 2000 survey found 91% of leaders expect to spend more on AI in 2026, mostly 20-40% more, yet two-thirds lack visibility into their model and API spend -- a sign of the gap between vanguard companies and the middle of the pack.
* **Switching is becoming normal**: A third of OpenAI customers in Arcana's survey said they'd be open to switching, frequently naming Anthropic, and 43% of GitHub Copilot customers were open to moving, generally naming Claude Code.
* **The top 1% of AI spenders are in a different universe**: On Ramp, the median firm spends $16.32 per employee per month while top firms spend over $10,000 -- about 625 times as much. Databricks sees the same pattern: a $2.49 median user against a 99th-percentile user spending nearly $700 a week, a 281x gap.
* **Coding remains king**: 72% of AI spend on Ramp comes from engineering teams, and a third of Arcana's Global 2000 respondents are piloting AI coding tools, the largest pilot group of any category.
* **Anthropic has the enterprise momentum on every scoreboard**: Just under 45% of US businesses on Ramp pay Anthropic versus 40.6% for OpenAI, Anthropic takes 68.8% of proprietary model tokens on Unity Gateway, and 81% of Arcana respondents named it as an exciting AI vendor.
* **Customer service is AI's fastest-growing business use case**: Customer support teams' share of AI spend on Ramp jumped from 0.8% in June to 13.3% in November, and 38% of Arcana's Global 2000 respondents already use AI customer service.
* **The reports don't agree on everything**: Ramp token spend grew 1.05% in August, 0.22% in September and 1.65% in October -- compounding to about 22% a year, a real slowdown but still inside Arcana's expected 20-40% range. Frontier models' share of Ramp spend rose to 69.5%, yet Fable's token share on Databricks fell from 5.5% to 0.6% while Opus 5.5 climbed from 7.2% to 37.9% of tokens in six weeks -- suggesting Opus 5.5 is simply leaving Fable 5.2 without a place.
* **Agents got real in 2026, just not for average enterprises**: Arcana ranks autonomous agents fourth on its heat index of what companies are excited about, but dead last on its adoption index of what is actually deployed.
* **Nvidia's board appears to shut the door on a Jensen Huang comeback**: Despite a 180,000-signature petition backed by Elon Musk and Tim Sweeney, shareholder letters from Altimeter's Brad Gerstner and Gavin Baker, and Trump saying he "would not mind" a return, sources told CNBC the board is focused on the Wachtell, Lipton investigation into whether Huang's conduct compromised national security -- and is not discussing bringing him back. Scott Galloway told CNN the odds are "almost zero."
* **Altman says the AI industry will be fine without Jensen**: Speaking at Semafor's event, Altman argued revenues and demand are strong enough for the industry to stand on its own, even though OpenAI itself relied on a $100 billion Nvidia guarantee for its Ohio facility. The Wall Street Journal reports Huang was already pulling back on commitments, telling neocloud bosses in Berlin: "I don't even need you guys. I could just give all this capacity to Elon." Interim CEO Colette Kress is expected to rein in data center backstops further.
* **Commerce finally moves on chip diversion**: A long-awaited rule requires export licenses for advanced AI chips going to data center operators in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, with existing facilities given 90 days to comply. The rule states it was written by and at the direction of Treasury and the National Security Council -- Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pushed it through with Marco Rubio's support amid a public feud with Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick that Trump declined to resolve by firing anyone.
* **Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic set to be named AI 'national champions'**: The Commerce Department is expected to name its first cohort under a year-old program, bringing expedited export licenses, government support for international business, and access to federal financing, with a core goal of bundling chips, models and products for international sales.
* **OpenAI's embedded safety evaluators come with strings attached**: OpenAI signed agreements with Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire for "deep access" to internal models, with Apollo embedding a six-person team for four months to test whether models subvert alignment and control mechanisms. But OpenAI can withhold information, selects which staff evaluators may speak with, keeps evaluators off Slack, and retains redaction rights -- Apollo itself said the pilot's access and disclosure rights "don't yet meet our bar for full embedded evaluations," while DeepMind's Neel Nanda and ex-OpenAI researcher Steven criticized the agreement for falling short of the employee-like access Altman promised.
* **A banking outage deepens the 'something doesn't add up' mood**: Logins, ATMs and transfers failed across JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Zelle, US Bank and others, traced back to Chase's back-end systems. Chase blamed a software bug in on-premises hardware and said it was not cyber-related, but after the Microsoft outage and the FAA grounding, public skepticism of the denials keeps growing.

The convergent story across all three datasets is stronger than the divergent one: model competition is forcing prices down, enterprises are getting comfortable switching providers, and the leaders are using far more AI without paying proportionally more. For enterprises, the practical takeaway is that you probably have much more negotiating leverage than you're exerting -- competition is vicious and there are deals to be had, but only if you've built the infrastructure to actually switch.
