# Summary: Why Muse Wonder Is Dividing AI Users

[The AI Daily Brief - Why Muse Wonder Is Dividing AI Users ](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/Why-Muse-Wonder-Is-Dividing-AI-Users-e3qf7l6)

On October 12, 2026, Meta released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full -- its first true frontier-class models, built by the Superintelligence Lab Alexandr Wang restructured a year ago -- and host Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW) examines why the release split AI users almost immediately. The benchmarks say Meta is back at the frontier for the first time in years, but hours of real engineering work told many serious builders a very different story. Underneath the divided reviews sits the episode's deeper dividing line: Zuckerberg isn't signing pacing-the-frontier letters, isn't building what he sees as a machine god, and told the Financial Times that model development is about to get faster from here.

* **Meta is back in the frontier race**: after 2025 headlines about extraordinary spending to poach researchers and the internal strife that followed, the Muse Spark models made Meta competitive but not state-of-the-art; Muse Wonder and Wonder Full (internally codenamed Watermelon, trained at the gigawatt-scale Prometheus data center) are meant to compete head-on with, as Chief AI Officer Alexandr Wang put it, the big boys.
* **The benchmarks say frontier**: Wonder Full scored 79% on DeepSuite 1.1 -- nearly 5 points ahead of GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5.5 -- was just 2 points behind the frontier rivals on Terminal Bench 4.0, topped Meta's internal Personal Superintelligence Index, and hit state-of-the-art on Harvey Lab legal work, Val's finance agent, and Automation Bench.
* **Artificial Analysis agrees, with a caveat**: it scored Wonder Full at 56 and Wonder at 52 on its Intelligence Index -- tied for second with Sonnet 5.5, two points behind Opus 5.5, and ahead of Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra -- calling it a material gain over Muse Spark 1.3 just six weeks after that model shipped.
* **Frontier price to match**: at $4.52 per task, Muse Wonder is nearly 3x the cost of Muse Spark 1.3 -- about $1 per task more than GPT-6 Astra or Groq 4.7, and about $1.50 cheaper per task than Opus 5.5.
* **The 3D backlash**: on the viral lawnmower-simulator-style 3D tests that dominate X, Wonder flopped badly -- "very clear that Muse is not anywhere near close to frontier in 3D tasks" -- and NLW goes further, arguing it is extremely net negative for the industry that frontier labs feel forced to optimize for flashy 3D demos because that is what X judges them on.
* **Meta's response was unusually engaged**: Wang conceded "we did not explicitly train our model on 3D demo games... in retrospect, we probably should have," replied to individual critics, and on the naming complaint answered "fair feedback, DMing you to understand more."
* **On real engineering work, reviews split hard**: Every's senior-engineer benchmark -- rewriting a vibe-coded slop database from scratch -- scored Muse Wonder at 52%, far behind even GPT-6-1 Sol at 71%; researchers running real programming tasks said the quality itself was bad, and at least one prominent builder went back to Codex and Claude Code after five hours.
* **Sharing a name hurt both models**: a recurring pattern was good experiences with Wonder Full and bad ones with the slimmed-down Wonder -- and because they share a name, the lesser model dragged the better one's reputation; several argued the two should have launched at different times.
* **Usage plans burned fast**: users reported plans consumed alarmingly quickly (a five-hour limit dinged 13% in under ten minutes, some forced to upgrade to the $200 Muse Code plan mid-task); Wang said a bug may have been miscounting usage, but whatever the cause it hurt first impressions.
* **The believers**: Theo put Wonder Full in his S tier alongside Opus 5.5 after it spotted an existing PR and recommended merging that instead of duplicating work; Charlie Holtz said it one-shot a real security vulnerability other agents missed; Clare Vo's category breakdown found it bad at front-end design and most writing but first of eight frontier models on agentic tasks; and one writer (handle BoneGPT) captured the affection some feel: "There aren't really benchmarks for soulfulness, but Muse has it... Meta is going to win consumer agents in a landslide."
* **Built for agents that act on your behalf**: the two headline capabilities are active memory -- tracking what matters to you across conversations over days, weeks, and months -- and active focus, which keeps a task going when it gets hard, tries other approaches, and recovers from errors, aimed squarely at Muse's long-horizon failures like doom-looping on Hilton's booking site.
* **The personal agent problem**: power users currently juggle Instinct, Muse, GrokBot, and Dots for different use cases, but NLW argues the context-switching cost won't scale -- personal assistants get more useful in proportion to what they know about you, and most people will struggle to give even one assistant that access, let alone half a dozen.
* **A coordinated "professional agents" message**: Zuckerberg, Wang, and the AI at Meta account all explicitly paired personal and professional agents -- which may mean Meta wants enterprise deals, or more likely that it knows the one personal agent consumers truly invest in will inevitably do professional work; Citrini Research sees Meta's real shot as agents for small business owners already living on Facebook and Instagram.
* **The delta is the story**: Muse launched 34 days ago and Meta has now joined the frontier; Ethan Mollick found the pace more impressive than the model, markets agreed with Meta up about 4% on the news, and with Gemini 4 Argon still unreleased, the US is back to five-ish frontier labs after coding agents had narrowed the field to two.
* **Zuckerberg's counter-signals**: he never signed the Pacing the Frontier letter, countered with his essay "The Future Is for Everyone," and told the FT Meta has reached the frontier and is "already working on that next step up," that development "probably feel like it'll go quite a bit faster from here," and he declined to give a P(doom) number -- framing AI as "a technology that can be engineered with normal guardrails" rather than an autonomous force.
* **The fundamental dividing line**: OpenAI and Anthropic are either trying to, or feel they inevitably are, building machine gods that inspire awe; Meta is trying to build what it views as just a normal technology -- who's right or wrong remains the debate, but that is the real division in frontier AI right now.
* **Headlines -- the labs are racing on speed**: Anthropic is serving Opus 5.5 at a verified 285 tokens per second on its upgraded Fast Mode (not included in regular subscriptions, as some found out the hard way), while OpenAI's docs suggest a 280 tok/s theoretical max for GPT-6-1 Sol at a 50% price premium -- both expensive and niche today, but latency shifts may change how we interact with models.
* **Headlines -- Fable 5.5 sightings**: users report queries being routed away from Opus 5.5 to a new model with impressive results, with both Opus- and Fable-style prompts affected, raising the possibility of a double-headed release timed a few weeks ahead of Anthropic's IPO -- and the next cycle will test whether the labs pace the frontier with mid-size releases like Opus and Sol or unleash another round of acceleration.
* **Headlines -- a Sora revival**: The Information reports OpenAI is in talks to license the mothballed Sora model (shut down in April as the prime example of its "side quests" purge) to a stealth startup founded by former Sora lead Bill Peebles with ex-Disney chairman Jeffrey Katzenberg and former Dropbox CFO Sujay Jaswa; weight access is unclear but customization would be deep.
* **Headlines -- Amazon's $7B Decart bet**: the WSJ reports Amazon is negotiating to acquire world-model startup Decart at a $7B valuation -- a 75% premium on its $4B May mark -- for its hardware-agnostic DOS inference stack, built with early access to Amazon's Trainium3 chips, which claims up to 8x faster inference at one-eighth the cost; if it works at scale it gives AWS a huge edge in a world where hardware efficiency is the benchmark that matters.
* **Headlines -- NVIDIA assembles the West's open-model stack**: the FT reports NVIDIA -- already an $800M investor in Reflection AI, valued at $25B in March -- is exploring further investment or a full acqui-hire after Reflection's Beam debut (which beat NVIDIA's own Nemotron among US open models); at that valuation it would top NVIDIA's $20B Groq purchase, following its Hugging Face acquisition and Poolside and Essential AI acqui-hires.
* **Headlines -- Microsoft's Maia 300**: Nadella announced Microsoft's biggest custom-silicon bet to date, claiming 40% more tokens per watt than leading GPU-based hardware, with multi-gigawatt Maia deployments starting next year making it the primary inference system for Microsoft's own products -- everyone now offers their own models, silicon, cloud, and applications, and the open question is what constraint (distribution, data moat, or power) margins get built around.

When you step back, 2026 began with three US frontier labs, coding agents' dominance narrowed that to two, and Muse Wonder puts it back to five-ish -- though with more powerful models reportedly just behind closed doors at OpenAI and Anthropic, the duopoly may reassert itself soon. For now, Meta is fully back in the game, and it is playing a different game than everyone else: no pacing pledges, no machine-god theology, just a normal technology -- built faster than anyone expected.
