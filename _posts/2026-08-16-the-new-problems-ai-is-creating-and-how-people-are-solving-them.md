# Summary: The New Problems AI Is Creating (And How People Are Solving Them)

[AI Daily Brief - The New Problems AI Is Creating (And How People Are Solving Them)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/The-New-Problems-AI-Is-Creating-And-How-People-Are-Solving-Them-e3nff4j)

Host Nathaniel Whittemore argues that the real story of the past year is not the capability race but the second-order problems agentic AI has created inside companies: AI slop, runaway token costs, and the slow erosion of the expertise needed to supervise the machines. A year ago enterprises were still debating whether AI would matter; now the questions have flipped from "if" to "how" -- how to police lazy AI writing with policies rather than bans, how to treat tokens like allocable capital rather than SaaS seats, and how to keep a pipeline of experts alive when AI eats the grunt work that builds them. The episode's thesis is that organizations are not the bumbling laggards the media paints but are already working through these challenges in public, one shared playbook at a time.

* **EY: the productivity boom is not going to be immediate**: In "Four AI misconceptions that deserve greater scrutiny," EY (Chief Economist Gregory Daco and EY-Parthenon's Rye Butman) argues the first stage of any technology revolution is infrastructure build-out -- as it took roughly five decades for electricity and a close-to-a-decade for computers to show up in aggregate productivity, AI's gains are being absorbed by the messy work of integrating new agentic workflows with human oversight. The result is a transitional phase of new work, not a clean one-to-one swap of humans for agents.

* **AI is not nearly free -- and the SaaS math broke**: Every prompt burns tokens, compute, and electricity, so AI has shifted from a one-time license investment into a recurring operating expense, and agentic AI makes it behave more like a new type of labor than a seat-per-month subscription. The media frames token budgets and usage caps as panicked catch-up by naive enterprises, but the host argues serious organizations are deliberately architecting which models, structures, and access levels go to which problems -- and pairing caps with pathways for people to apply for more or prove they deserve it.

* **"AI will make labor redundant" is a crock**: Whittemore says blaming 40% or more of layoffs on AI has been a convenient, market-accepted excuse pushed by a few over-promoting lab leaders and executives who needed cover, and AI genuinely does not make labor redundant. That story is now failing as layoff-blaming companies increasingly face pressure to re-hire the people they cut.

* **The anti-slop "immune system" is coming online**: Platforms and companies are responding to the flood of poor AI writing -- Substack is adding AI detectors (which the host remains skeptical of) and LinkedIn is testing a button to flag posts that "seem like AI slop," and the more such systems appear the weaker the incentive to be lazy.

* **Clay's AI writing policy bans laziness, not AI**: Co-founder Varun Anand published the company policy, originally for engineering and expanded company-wide, around four principles: stand behind every idea and sentence; treat writing as thinking, since circumventing it leaves a poorer understanding of the subject; spend more time writing a document than readers will consume it; and remember longer is not better -- if a doc came from a short prompt, consider just sharing the prompt. The post drew 8,377 LinkedIn reactions, a strong sign the model will show up at many more organizations.

* **OpenAI CFO Sarah Friar: build an AI-native finance function**: In "What building an AI-native finance function taught me," Friar describes moving past static spreadsheets toward a real-time "zero-day close" and continuously automated forecasting, with lessons like giving everyone access while creating a reason to use it -- bottom-up experimentation plus top-down strategy. Her scorecard replaces seats and tokens with value per unit of intelligence: did AI complete work that mattered, what did it cost including employee review and rework, was the result good enough to use, and did it help the team move faster or make a better decision.

* **Professionals are becoming builders**: OpenAI research Friar cites suggests around 40% of finance professionals' specialized AI use involves work outside traditional finance and 22% involves engineering-related tasks, with her team building custom dashboards in ChatGPT and Codex. The upskilling question, in her framing, is how to let knowledge workers carry their existing expertise further with these tools.

* **Section CEO Greg Shove: do not shrink the budget**: Shove's advice to CEOs is that token-maxxing is stupid but so is token-minimizing -- invest in transformation and accept that you will not have the full picture for one to two years, pick a "lighthouse" team and 10x the investment, and avoid the 12-month stall by asking exactly which teams are blocked and what would let them work differently.

* **BCG: own the harness, not the model**: Global Chair Rich Lesser describes a shift in the CEO question from "which model should we buy?" to committing to an organization-level "harness" -- what BCG calls the enterprise cortex of IP, data, key business rules, and codified process knowledge -- that can run any model or combination of models while preserving the company's context, tools, guardrails, and governance.

* **The quiet risk of de-skilling**: Pointing to a BCG paper, the episode warns that the erosion of judgment, critical thinking, and problem-framing can spread across an entire workforce while adoption dashboards look great -- half of surveyed leaders already report seeing it, over 60% expect it to be a real threat within three to five years, and fewer than one in five employees say they feel confident using AI today. Confidence, not token usage, is the better proxy for real adoption, and it is built by reinforcing the right behaviors every time.

* **KPMG: overspending on technology, underspending on talent**: KPMG's Adaptability Report finds executives are twice as likely to increase investment in new technology as in employee training, 57% cite performance and efficiency as a top priority while under 10% prioritize stronger workforce training, and leaders who had increased workforce investment reported 20% or more revenue growth at a 37% rate versus 25% overall.

* **The Tragedy of the Cognitive Commons**: A paper surfaced by X handle Zara Zhang names a coming bind -- checking an AI's mistakes requires deep expertise, deep expertise comes from years of grunt work, and grunt work is the first thing AI automates. Each firm acts rationally by eliminating junior roles, while the collective result is a profession that can no longer catch AI's errors because it never learned to do the work, leaving the open question of who will check AI's homework in 15 years.

The takeaway is that the in-the-trenches conversation about AI has matured far faster and more honestly than the media coverage suggests. The useful question is no longer whether AI is real but how to do it well: allocate scarce intelligence like capital, police slop with norms rather than bans, measure value per unit of expertise rather than seats consumed, and keep the human expertise pipeline alive while adopting the technology -- with companies increasingly publishing their playbooks in the open so that everyone does not have to solve each problem alone.
