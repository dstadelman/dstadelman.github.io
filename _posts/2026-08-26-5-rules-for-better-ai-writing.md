# Summary: 5 Rules for Better AI Writing

[The AI Daily Brief - 5 Rules for Better AI Writing](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/5-Rules-for-Better-AI-Writing-e3nuhsk)

The August 26, 2026 episode of the AI Daily Brief turns on a single viral moment: hedge-fund legend Stanley Druckenmiller published a Wall Street Journal op-ed slamming Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, and the discourse instantly detached from the economics to interrogate the fact that the piece was unmistakably AI-written. When Druckenmiller answered "of course I used AI" and the Journal's opinion editor backed him, the show reframes the question from "is AI writing legitimate?" to "how do we make it actually good?" -- offering five maxims plus a use-case-by-use-case crib sheet for emails, memos, social, marketing copy, and op-eds.

* **The op-ed that started it**: Druckenmiller -- whose Duquesne fund famously had zero losing years from 1981 to 2010 -- published "Let the Bond Market Speak," a full-throated critique of Bessent's Treasury bond-buyback push, and within days of its release the substance was no longer what anyone was talking about ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/25/scott-bessent-stanley-druckenmiller-ai)).
* **The AI-isms were unmistakable**: the piece was replete with "it's not this, it's that" turnarounds ("that isn't a crisis, it's an invoice"), and AI detector Pangram put the odds of it being machine-generated at 100% ([The Wrap](https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/tech/stanley-druckenmiller-wsj-op-ed-written-by-ai/)).
* **No apology -- a flex**: rather than confessing sheepishly, Druckenmiller said "Bro, of course I used AI," added that he is "not embarrassed" by it, and that he "write[s] everything using AI now for the same reason I use a calculator when I do math problems" ([NOTUS](https://www.notus.org/media/stanley-druckenmillers-wsj-op-ed-bessent-ai)).
* **The WSJ has his back**: opinion editor Paul Gigot argued AI "is a fact of modern life" and that the question is whether the piece "reflects an author's original argument" -- the Journal's clear position that the sincerity of the ideas beats the human flavor of the words.
* **Ghostwriters were the old norm**: Bloomberg's Joe Weisenthal noted executives routinely had op-eds written by underlings without controversy, and journalist Sharon Goldman confirmed ghostwriting dozens of executive op-eds from a single conversation and some notes, never wanting a byline.
* **The disclosure fight**: All In's Jason Calacanis called publishing an AI-written piece without a first-sentence disclosure "unforgivable for a public figure," while co-host Chamath Palihapitiya pushed back that it is "a dumb new form of virtue signaling" -- if he puts his name behind it, it is his opinion.
* **Art or tool**: Spectra Markets' Brent Donnelly framed the crux as whether writing is an art or a functional communication medium like code, and the sharpest critique conceded Druckenmiller and Gigot are "100% true descriptively" but read the stance as contempt for the op-ed format and its audience.
* **AI does the middle**: CNBC's Deirdre Bosa revived Balaji Srinivasan's "AI doesn't do end-to-end, it does middle-to-middle" -- the beginning (what is the idea, what are you trying to say?) and the end (is this any good, do I buy it?) need a human, while the middle (research, organize, draft, tighten, poke holes) is AI's lane.
* **Rule 1 -- different writing, different rules**: an email, a strategy memo, a LinkedIn post, and an op-ed try to achieve different things, so "can or should AI write this" is mostly the wrong question -- how you engage AI has to differ by context.
* **Rule 2 -- purity dies, quality doesn't**: the purity-test phase will go the way of the dodo, but that only raises the bar on outputs -- easier inputs do not relax the burden on the finished writing, they sharpen it.
* **Rule 3 -- quality reads as effort**: the backlash tracked perceived effort, because the AI-isms were so glaring and so fixable that it read as not caring enough to retouch two sentences -- and if he didn't care that much, how much thought went into the argument underneath?
* **Rule 4 -- longer isn't better**: "workslop" exists because recent models are better at saying a lot than saying the right thing; Pascal's 17th-century "I've made this longer because I have not had time to make it shorter" still holds, and the eye-bleeding first wave of AI length is not the direction.
* **Rule 5 -- writing is thinking**: choosing the thesis, the supporting arguments, and the narrative is an exercise whose real product is the substance under the words, not the words themselves -- the constant risk in AI writing is quietly outsourcing that thinking, which is so easy to fall into that it must hover in your field of view.
* **Emails: safe, but dictation may beat it**: prose quality is rarely judged on email and the units are small, so the intellectual-outsourcing risk is low -- and for interactive Slack-style mail, dictation with something like Whisperflow can edge out a full AI draft.
* **Meeting notes: safe, but watch the exhaustiveness trap**: summarization is compression, not composition, and the failure mode is an AI that is great at reciting what everyone said but weak at surfacing the one or two things that actually mattered -- so outsource the summary, then layer your own sense of what mattered on top.
* **Strategy memos: sneakily bad for AI**: unless you are hyper-precise about the strategy and what it should not communicate, AI drifts off the rails into generic training-data advice -- so think the strategy through exhaustively, let AI support the outlining, then let it write the words once the outline is tight.
* **Social and marketing: tuned but capped**: AI does a one-line X post better than a full LinkedIn essay, but social now rewards engagement and human signal that automated posting can't supply, and marketing copy is "annoyingly bad" -- the host's pipeline of disaggregated episode posts increased sharing before slamming into its wall within weeks, and his pet failure is models turning his own corrections into copy (a headline reading "We assume nothing about the reader").
* **Op-eds: the effort sandwich**: the whole point is to convince, so get extremely clear on the thesis and the supporting points before turning it to AI ("the five-paragraph essay from grade school, baby"), let it draft, then go back and scrub the obvious AI-isms -- readers can tell low effort instantly and they switch off.
* **Writing is already the dominant use**: OpenAI's recently released enterprise data shows the preponderance of ChatGPT usage across almost every department is writing -- recruiting, marketing, customer support, legal, sales, policy -- which is why the show treats AI writing as a fact of life rather than a threat.

AI writing is going nowhere and will unlock communication for people who never leaned on prose before -- but it will not erase the one thing readers always detect: laziness in the work reads as laziness in the work -- and there are no shortcuts to doing a thing well, even when the tools let you do it well faster.
