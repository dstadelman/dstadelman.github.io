# Summary: How the Most AI-Pilled Company in the World Works

[AI Daily Brief - How the Most AI-Pilled Company in the World Works](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-the-Most-AI-Pilled-Company-in-the-World-Works-e3red3r)

Jacob Sansbury, founder of the 10-person game-creation startup Spawn, published a playbook in a blog post titled "How I Built the Most AI-Pilled Company in the World," detailing two years of building a company where a persistent fleet of AI agents does nearly everything except set long-term vision. Spawn spent $3.1 million on tokens last month -- 30 times its payroll -- making it one of Anthropic's biggest customers, and its fleet shipped 77,000 code changes in 30 days. The headlines edition covers legal AI startup Crosby bidding for the assets of fallen Wall Street firm Weil, Gotshal & Manges, tech earnings exposing a rift between hyperscalers and investors, and a New York Times report that Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang was the target of a Chinese intelligence asset.

* **Spawn's agent fleet is permanent, not disposable**: each agent has a name, identity, memory, real system permissions, and ownership of a function -- game engine, bug triage, finance -- and they even get Sundays off; daily, the fleet spawns thousands of helper agents for granular work while the human team uses a modified Claude Code for bigger-picture steering.
* **The economics are inverted**: $3.1 million in monthly token spend versus roughly $100K on payroll (30x), running on 5,000 CPUs, 17 TB of RAM, and 150 GPUs on AWS with another 60% surge capacity on standby; founder Sansbury initially thought he was using agents wrong because his AI bills dwarfed those of friends inside big tech.
* **None of it worked until Anthropic's Fable model**: Fable gave them 500x the shippable changes and forced a complete rebuild of operations around it; Sansbury's model comparison -- OpenAI's models game their own checks, Google's don't stay coherent long, xAI's aren't smart enough yet, and Anthropic's stay coherent and do the work but don't take enough risk.
* **Spawn killed pull requests**: agents were spending most of their time telling each other what they were doing (double the agents, roughly quadruple the chatter), and since agents understand the entire system deeply, the coordination overhead had no purpose -- changes now go directly into the codebase, with a fixer agent spawned whenever something breaks.
* **The approach is surprisingly resilient**: of 123,000 fleet changes over the past year, only about 1,000 were reverted; test suites run every 15 minutes, the fleet must have everything in line hourly, and not shipping for an hour counts as an outage -- in one typical incident a bug was detected 8 minutes after hitting a player, diagnosed a minute later, and fixed 11 minutes after that.
* **The feedback loop skips humans entirely**: players' in-game agents (Savi) detect bugs and file reports automatically, a small model triages each one, and routes it to the right fleet agent -- making the product close to self-healing software.
* **Founder taste became precedent**: after waking up to 700 pending decisions, Spawn formalized two years of decision logs into principles applied by AI judges; in their first eight days (from October 6) the judges made 11,000+ decisions -- about 7,300 from human precedent with an audit trail, 2,200 kicked back to agents, 1,800 escalated to humans -- while Sansbury personally decided only 27, and daily shipped changes jumped from about 1,000 to over 7,000.
* **What's left for humans**: taste (playing with the product, telling Claude no even when something works), deleting work that new models make redundant (Sansbury scrapped six months of work covering 70% of the engine in June), and supplying entropy -- ambition the models won't propose, like the prompt behind their best build: how do you make a world a thousand times the size of GTA load on a phone in one second?
* **Why big companies will struggle to copy it**: none of it works unless you close the loop, and closing the loop takes risk appetite -- you have to let agents break production; the host adds two more reasons: the system is nearly impossible to retrofit onto an existing company, and Spawn's self-healing depends on a fast user feedback loop most products don't naturally have.
* **Five transferable lessons**: give agents ongoing responsibility instead of one-off tasks; let the work carry the context through a shared, current record; make results observable; turn decisions into precedent so each one builds the canon; and keep human attention on direction, because ambition and long-horizon thinking don't come naturally to agents.
* **Crosby bids for Weil, Gotshal & Manges**: the 95-year-old white-shoe firm that handled the Lehman Brothers and Enron bankruptcies is collapsing -- the New York Times blamed a partner exodus, a failed succession plan, and a scuttled merger with Willkie Farr & Gallagher, not AI -- and legal AI startup Crosby (last valued at $400 million, ~50 lawyers, backed by Sequoia, Bain Capital Ventures, Lux Capital, and Index Ventures) submitted a letter of intent for the trademark, brand, and internal data, hoping to hire some of its 1,200 lawyers.
* **A law firm's archive used to be worth zero -- now it's RL training data**: precedent documents, contract templates, and workflows for elite bankruptcy work could be worth tens of millions to the right AI buyer; restructuring lawyer Harneet K. noted Google recently bid $10 million for Spirit Airlines' far less specialized operational data, and quipped: "the AI native law firm is about to become just a law firm."
* **The AI trade's cracks spread**: AWS grew 39%, its best in almost five years, but Amazon missed on overall revenue and net income and is $42.4 billion cash-flow negative over twelve months; Intel's data center business grew 78% yet its foundry posted a $1.9 billion quarterly operating loss and the stock crashed 23%; Google fell 12%, losing half a trillion in market cap -- AI revenue is red-hot, but legacy businesses subsidizing the buildout are slowing.
* **Trump's Grok chats become a records fight**: after a Time profile reported the president asked Grok about forcibly removing Venezuela's President Nicolas Maduro and came away thinking Grok was "ingenious," Reps. Jamie Raskin and Andre Garcia demanded all chatbot logs be preserved as U.S. government property, calling reliance on the technology "deeply troubling."
* **NYT: Jensen Huang targeted by a Chinese intelligence asset**: Alice Huang (also known as Huang Le), an executive at Singapore-based data center company Megaspeed, was under U.S. surveillance on suspicion of diverting AI chips to China and passing information to Beijing, and surveillance revealed a close relationship with Huang; the president was briefed about a year ago and took no action, and Huang never received a defensive briefing; Nvidia says Megaspeed received only ordinary business information and the diversion claims were "thoroughly investigated and debunked."
* **KPMG/UT Austin study on "AI amplifiers"**: researchers studied 500+ early-career professionals and found similar skills don't guarantee similar outcomes with AI -- the best performers amplified AI's value by guiding, evaluating, and refining its outputs, defined by how they worked with AI rather than what they knew.

The primary takeaway: truly AI-native means tearing out the systems built for humans rather than speeding them up -- Spawn's two-year playbook shows what's possible when agents own real functions, the feedback loop closes without humans, and decisions become precedent, while remaining genuinely hard to copy because it requires risk appetite, a greenfield build, and a product with a naturally fast feedback loop.
