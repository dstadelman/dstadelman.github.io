# Summary: 10 Ways to Think Bigger with Opportunity AI
[The AI Daily Brief - 10 Ways to Think Bigger with Opportunity AI](https://d3ctxlq1ktw2nl.cloudfront.net/staging/2026-8-13/431794046-44100-2-7fa581c7906a.mp3)

Host NLW reframes OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra release -- a model advanced coders are increasingly abandoning for its mood swings and cost, while everyone else stumbles into new capabilities like video and 3D -- as the clearest sign yet of where AI value is heading: not doing your existing work a little better, but unlocking things you never considered doing. Framed as ten ways and shipped as a full dozen "thought starters" in the companion web experience, the episode leans on his efficiency-AI versus opportunity-AI framework to show how knowledge workers and solopreneurs can use frontier models to build, market, and sell in ways that were not possible before.

* **Efficiency AI vs. Opportunity AI**: Efficiency AI makes current work faster, cheaper, and leaner; Opportunity AI unlocks entirely new capabilities. That distinction has mostly been a mindset, but Astra is one of the first models where the difference is visible in the model itself. Because efficiency gains become table stakes, the companies that win -- his argument goes -- are the ones actively hunting new opportunities, even ones orthogonal to what they do today.
* **Astra is the "smartest and dumbest" model for coding**: Power users on X described it as the smartest and dumbest model they've worked with, citing "mood swings" and "absurd shortcuts" that land somewhere technically working. OpenCode's Dax said a portion of the team has gone back to Sol, with Astra able to do novel things but carrying an effective spend that has roughly doubled -- hard to justify for coding. Yet the initial buzz around Astra is not about coding at all but about brand-new capabilities like video editing and 3D design and modeling.
* **The blank-page problem, and the shortcut for escaping it**: People do not walk around carrying a complete inventory of all the things they might make, so "go find an opportunity" usually produces a blank page. The practical shortcut he lands on is to notice what people in other roles and jobs are doing that strikes you as cool, and port that capability into your own work. Much of what looks like "novel" opportunity AI is in fact something other people already do -- you just have not seen it from the inside of your role.
* **Thought starter 1 -- marketing people can actually play**: Interactive and playable marketing, not just static visual, print, or video. His examples: a short game where adding team members to a project also piles on coordination work (making an advisory's core point experiential), a five-minute fictional launch where a consultant's prospect feels the cost of cross-team confusion, and a "make this awkward apartment work" challenge that lets a customer exercise taste against real space. Games implicate the audience's agency instead of treating them as a receiver, and what used to require a full dev team is now a weekend solopreneur experiment.
* **Thought starter 2 -- your own video production pipeline**: The AI Daily Brief's clips are not made by a dedicated clipping product but by a custom Claude-run pipeline that ingests the script and raw footage and ships the finished video, and he suspects it barely scratches the surface. The homework he gives: have an AI write a 60-second script, record it on an iPhone, then hand the clip to Codex or Claude Code to design a visual motif, transitions, and layered graphics, and wrap it into a drop-in pipeline where your only job is to add the source footage. The thesis is that these models could democratize video the way coding agents democratized software.
* **Thought starter 3 -- product demos a buyer can explore**: Give the customer control over how they understand the product, so a demo organizes itself around actions (open this, isolate that component, inspect the result) and the buyer's own question determines the path, rather than every prospect sitting through a single pre-ordered pitch. The diagnostic question: what does a customer need to be able to inspect for themselves before your product makes sense to them?
* **Thought starter 4 -- proposals clients can shape**: Every proposal already contains an invisible model -- assumptions about scope, parallelism, resources, and timing. Expose parts of that model so a client can trade off "we want it sooner" against "we can finish sooner if the team attends more often." Done well this is efficiency and opportunity AI at once, because it is a new way of working with clients and simultaneously collapses the back-and-forth latency in every negotiation.
* **Thought starter 5 -- simulators for business decisions**: "We need another hire" usually hides several separate disagreements (the process is too slow, work is unevenly distributed, or someone expects a demand surge), so people argue about the conclusion while starting from different assumptions. A simulator forces the team to specify those starting conditions and makes the consequence of each assumption inspectable -- like asking whether the call still holds if demand rises more slowly, or if training a new person takes longer. He ties this to his Multiplayer AI Sprint, the push to put shared agents at the center of teams rather than owned by individuals.
* **Thought starters 6, 7, 8, 9 (3D, customer films, practice environments, physical prototyping)**: Astra's headline strength is that it can operate in 3D -- think Blender-driven walkthroughs of Zillow listings and learning experiences where rotating a real 3D object changes what you learn. Related threads include turning existing customer material (interviews, recordings, screenshots) into mini-documentary films that let a viewer see both the problem and the fix, simulation-style practice environments where people rehearse difficult customer or management conversations and get immediate feedback on judgment, and prototyping physical products -- even an abstract teaching tool with removable pieces -- a category his "Make It Mine" companion panel exists to make concrete.
* **Thought starter 12 -- your expertise as a product**: Expert help is a conversation, but most of its value is invisible work -- gathering context, recognizing patterns, ruling out attractive-but-wrong options, gauging what someone is ready for next. The move is to give a specific chunk of that judgment a form someone can work through themselves, which is exactly what the interactive app he ships with the episode does. That scales you, works for teammates as much as clients, and doubles as efficiency AI wherever you spend your day repeating the same guidance to different people.

Because efficiency gains are quickly becoming table stakes while opportunity-driven capabilities compound, the strategic habit he is pushing is to keep asking what you might do now that you never could -- and when an idea feels abstract, point a frontier model (or a friend in a different role) at your own work to surface the concrete version of it. The goal of opportunity AI is simply to stretch the inventory you carry and to make "what could I build that's brand new?" a regular, cheaply-testable question instead of a once-a-decade gamble.
