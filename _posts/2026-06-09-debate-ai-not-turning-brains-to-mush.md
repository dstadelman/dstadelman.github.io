The "mush-brains" claim resurfaces every time technology forces people to reconsider what thinking looks like. Plato warned writing would destroy memory. We still write things down. Andy Clark, co-developer of the extended mind thesis, says human cognition has always been a hybrid system [1]. In a 2025 commentary in Nature Communications, he extends that framework to generative AI, describing humans as "natural-born cyborgs" who offload cognitive work onto external tools by nature [1]. The nervous system doesn't neatly separate internal memory from externally stored information the way we imagine it does [2]. We already use PACS for medical imaging, Git for code review, calculators for math -- all are extensions of the same architecture.

## AI as a Debate Partner

The mush-brains argument assumes AI only functions as an answer machine. It doesn't have to. A 2025 study from Ludwigsburg University and UNC Charlotte built an AI system specifically as a Socratic sparring partner [3]. Rather than providing answers, it pushed 97 undergraduates to defend evidence-based positions across the UN's Sustainable Development Goals. Sixty percent reported positive impact on their research memoranda. Fifty-two percent revised their memos with more evidence-based claims. Thirty-two percent considered perspectives they'd never entertained before [3]. The debate structure forced metacognitive reflection. The AI became an instrument of intellectual rigor, not a substitute for it.

Khan et al. found the same pattern [4]. Engaging with challenging counterarguments through AI-powered debate leads to more truthful answers than one-sided information retrieval. The outcome depends on design, not existence.

## AI Can Raise the Creative Floor

Wharton's research directly refutes the cognitive degradation thesis [5]: AI "supercharged" less-creative individuals, enabling them to produce work judged more novel and useful than before. This comes from the WHAIR webinar "How AI Shapes Creativity -- Expanding Potential or Narrowing Possibilities?" which examined research by Kartik Hosanagar, Anil Doshi, Oliver Hauser, and Léonard Boussioux [5].

Hosanagar's research also shows a caveat worth acknowledging: people who start with an AI suggestion get anchored to it, narrowing idea diversity [5]. The fix isn't to abandon AI. Generate initial ideas as a human. Let AI handle evaluation or refinement. The cognitive leveling effect weighs more heavily because it helps the people who need it most.

## Offloading Is What Expertise Is

Chess grandmasters don't calculate every move internally. Surgeons use checklists. Pilots use autopilot. The difference between expertise and exhaustion is knowing what to automate and what to keep internal.

A 2025 study by TRENDS Research & Advisory documents AI improving memory, attention, learning speed, and decision-making across populations [7]. Intelligent tutoring systems "address the unique learning styles and paces of each student" [7]. We already offload. The question is what cognitive muscle to exercise and what to automate.

## The MIT Findings Say More Than Their Headlines

Fifty-four participants wore 32-channel EEG sensors across four essays over several months [8]. Those using ChatGPT showed neural connectivity up to 55% lower than unaided writers, and the researchers termed this "cognitive debt" [8].

That's the headline. The qualifier is this: when those same participants switched to writing without AI in session four, their brain connectivity increased. The researchers wrote that "timing might be important" [8]. Brains respond to task demands. Scaffold the task and effort drops. Ask them to write alone and effort ramps back up. That's neuroplasticity, and it's working as neuroscience predicts.

## Harvard Experts Read the Same Data Differently

Dan Levy, Senior Lecturer at Harvard Kennedy School, states there is no such thing as "AI is good for learning" or "AI is bad for learning" [6]. No learning occurs unless the brain is actively making meaning [6].

Tina Grotzer, Principal Research Scientist at Harvard's Graduate School of Education, calls the comparison between human and AI cognition a false binary. She points to Antonio Damasio's work on somatic markers -- intuitive leaps that enable quick, informed reasoning -- and her own research showing kindergarteners using strategic information in ways that exceeded purely Bayesian approaches [6]. The brain's analogical reasoning is something AI demonstrably cannot replicate. The question isn't AI versus human cognition. It's what we achieve combining both.

## The 3R Principle: Peer-Reviewed, Not Preprint

Rossi, Fraccaro, and Manzotti published the "3R principle" (Results, Responses, Responsibility) in npj Artificial Intelligence in January 2026 [9]. Grounded in plasticity rules and ethical considerations, they found that neuroplasticity responds to how humans interact with AI. Active co-creation sustains or enhances cognitive function. Passive, uncritical reliance weakens activity-dependent brain plasticity [9].

Active versus disengagement. The distinction is about interaction design, not the tool itself.

## Data Shows Adoption, Not Collapse

If brains were genuinely deteriorating from AI exposure, we'd see measurable decline in cognitive performance metrics. What RAND Corporation's March 2026 report found instead: student AI adoption for homework accelerated from 48% to 62% between May and December 2025 [10]. This is a population integrating new tools, not retreating from thinking. Institutions like Western Michigan University have revised critical thinking frameworks to incorporate AI as a teaching instrument rather than abandon them [11].

## The Quality of the Evidence

The "mush brains" argument leans on a particular type of evidence. Much of what gets cited most is small, non-peer-reviewed preprints [8]. The Harvard Gazette piece itself acknowledges that the MIT study "is small and is not peer-reviewed" [6].

Alberto Romero's compilation of over 30 studies arrives at a conclusion that undermines the doom narrative: effects are bidirectional, contingent on design, context, and user agency [12]. There are structured responses to how AI is used. That's a design question, not a degradation thesis.
