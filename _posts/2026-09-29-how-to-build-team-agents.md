# Summary: How to Build Team Agents

[The AI Daily Brief - How to Build Team Agents](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Build-Team-Agents-e3pj5qj)

An Operator's Cut episode where guest Nufar Gaspar, an AI consultant and executive trainer who runs enterprise agent programs with Nathaniel Whittemore, maps the shift from solo agents to the shared "team agents" living in the work between people. The core claim is that 2026 made agents normal but kept them personal, while most of real work happens at the intersection of roles and departments -- and the most AI-forward companies are already consolidating that sprawl into fewer, broader agents with shared knowledge, one configuration, and a named owner. The episode argues getting there is not a tooling problem but a set of deliberate business decisions, and it walks through them with a single running example: a customer agent spanning sales, solution engineering, customer success, and delivery.

* **The problem team agents solve is organizational, not technical**: every company has the person who can't take vacation because they alone know how pricing exceptions or the flagship customer's setup works, and the second failure mode is work nobody fully owns -- a customer moving from sales to marketing to success, with promises made mid-deal surfacing weeks before renewal -- where siloed private agents often make it worse.
* **AI-forward companies follow a three-step pattern**: everyone builds their own agents, enough of them create "agent sprawl" (overlapping work, one-person maintenance, agents dying when the owner leaves), then some merge into fewer shared team agents; Every is cited as giving every employee an agent early in the year and moving to shared team agents by May, Sierra as merging specialist agents into one, and Shopify as running an internal team agent.
* **A working definition, and what is not one**: a team agent is one agent many people talk to, with shared knowledge, shared memory, and one configuration -- instructions, skills, access, and owner are all shared -- which distinguishes it from a company skill library, since a skill is a playbook for one task while a team agent carries the team's knowledge across diverse, ad-hoc and repeated work.
* **Not every agent should be shared -- there's a three-setting dial**: private agents (taste and access stay yours, e.g. a personal social media agent), shared knowledge with private agents (one maintained body of truth everyone points their own agent at, often the right and easiest first move, like salespeople each keeping a tuned prospecting agent but sharing one ideal-customer profile), and full team agents; agents should move along the dial, and repeated requests to borrow a private agent is the signal to promote it.
* **Four archetypes of team agents**: the expert agent (one person's know-how -- data questions, pricing and deal desk, compliance -- made available to everyone, which is what finally makes their vacation possible but triggers real job insecurity, so tread carefully); the common work agent (the tell being three people have built the same research, meeting prep, or content agent, and merging them forces the team to agree on the standard); the bridge agent (work flowing between roles where handoffs break -- and permissions become the make-or-break design problem); and the chief of staff agent (decisions, commitments, status, onboarding, where the hard question is defining exactly what it's allowed to learn from ongoing work).
* **Three signs a team agent is the wrong move**: when taste beats standards (individual voice is the point), when nobody can own and maintain the shared knowledge (the agent drifts within weeks, sometimes days), and when sharing complicates more than it simplifies; notably, sensitive data and high stakes are not on the list -- they're design questions, not disqualifiers.
* **The five core design decisions, walked through with the customer agent example**: what it does, where it lives, what it knows, what it can touch, and how you run it; the episode scopes by role (sales asks different questions than delivery), keeps one broad area of work to justify the agent's existence, and starts narrow with reading and drafting until the agent earns trust.
* **Where the agent lives, from simplest to most involved**: a shared folder of instructions the team's existing tools can point at; a vendor-hosted agent (Claude Tag in Slack, ChatGPT's workspace agents, Copilot, Notion and others offering shared agent spaces); or one you host yourself, e.g. an open-source agent like OpenClaw or Hermes on your own infrastructure or a custom harness -- with two follow-on choices: who can see each person's conversation with it, and where its learning is stored and who can read it.
* **What it knows is where team agents get hardest**: the knowledge pipeline runs collect (interview the experts, harvest what's already written), refine (merge sources, surface the contradictions, date everything, keep out what should never be shared), approve (each piece signed off by its owner, then assembled by the agent's owner), and maintain (decide what the agent may add to its own memory vs. what needs human review, and put a periodic "propose updates" review on a schedule -- because getting this wrong doesn't just fail your agent, it can "create a lot of havoc" across the company).
* **What it can touch is where team agents differ from private ones**: your own agent acts as you, but a team agent acts for many people -- decide whose access it uses (act as the asker, safest but most complex; its own service account sized to the job; or one person's login, which the episode advises against except for read-only unsensitive material), decide who is allowed to ask it (an agent with a pricing sheet in a 40-person channel with contractors suddenly gives all 40 access to the pricing sheet), decide where the answer lands (sensitive answers must go privately, not into a shared channel -- the episode notes current cloud implementations don't consider who else is in the room), and log who asked for what.
* **Anthropic's Claude Tag is the reference case for where this is heading**: launched in beta on Slack on Team and Enterprise plans, it works under its own service-account identity scoped per channel, with an admin controlling which tools and channels it can reach -- which is exactly why the "answer lands in the shared space" and "who can see whose conversation" questions the episode raises are already real, live design decisions rather than theoretical ones.
* **How you run it is what keeps it alive past the first month**: one owner (a named person or very small group who sets priorities and decides when two people ask for opposite things), clear rules of engagement (what it does and doesn't do, what it can see, who can see conversations, and, critically, how to correct it when it's wrong), putting team decisions and day-to-day context in places the agent can actually see, and continuous monitoring with re-run test questions -- plus being willing to retire the agent if it's not working.
* **A side ad spot that's genuinely on-thesis**: a KPMG and UT Austin field study of 523 early-career professionals, published in Harvard Business Review, found three performance profiles -- AI Amplifiers (50.1%) who outperformed the AI-only baseline by directing and refining the agent's work, AI Delegators (25.8%) who matched it, and AI Apprentices (24.1%) who came in below it -- with the surprise being that foundational skill measures didn't predict which group you'd land in, i.e. what separates the top performers is how they work with the AI, not what they know.
* **Which archetype to start with, and whether to build now at all**: the expert agent is the lowest-hanging fruit, with the cheapest early form being the "agentified internal knowledge hub" (a policy or company-FAQ chatbot) that lots of companies already run successfully; the bridge agent -- the episode's own customer-agent example -- is the most attractive in theory but the hardest to execute, which is why it typically comes later; and on the build-vs-wait question, the heavy lifting is configuration and knowledge curation, so pick the tooling that best fits your existing ecosystem, do the ground-truth agreement properly, and you're positioned to move onto whatever the next generation of vendor tools (Claude Tag-class and beyond) actually delivers.

The primary point of the episode is that team agents are the next wave of practical AI -- moving from agents that do your solo work to agents that carry the shared knowledge, context, and coordination that lives between your roles and your teammates -- and that building one well is less a technical problem than a set of deliberate, business-level decisions: what it does, where it lives, what it knows, what it can touch, and how you run it, all of which depend as much on the team agreeing on the ground truth as on the quality of the model or tool underneath.
