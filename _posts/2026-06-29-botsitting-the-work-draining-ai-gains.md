# Summary: Botsitting: The Work Draining AI Gains

[Nathaniel Whittemore - The AI Daily Brief](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/Botsitting-The-Work-Draining-AI-Gains-e3lapj5)

A new report from Glean's Work AI Institute on the Work AI Index 2026 exposes a widening paradox: while individual AI productivity gains are enormous, organizations as a whole are not seeing corresponding returns. The episode explores the hidden labor of "botsitting" and "bot-shitting" that is draining AI's promise at work.

* **The productivity paradox**: Glean surveyed 6,000 workers across the US, UK, and Australia in December 2025 and January 2026. Eighty-seven percent of digital workers use AI at work, with 75% saying it makes them more productive by saving about 11 hours per week. Yet only 13 percent say their organization is performing significantly better as a result.

* **Botsitting defined**: Workers spend an average of 6.4 hours weekly on botsitting -- the invisible labor of feeding AI missing context, checking its outputs, debugging mistakes, and cleaning up confident-but-wrong answers. Of that 6.4 hours, 2.3 hours went to feeding AI context, 2.2 hours to supervising outputs, and 1.7 hours to debugging.

* **Where AI time actually goes**: Glean categorized AI usage into three buckets: 36 percent of time on active work completion with AI, 27 percent on learning and building agents, and 37 percent on botsitting. Unproductive botsitting -- reloading context across multiple tools, comparing outputs because the first answer was not good enough -- is particularly draining.

* **Bot-shitting and cognitive offloading**: The report introduced "bot-shitting" -- workers shipping AI-generated work they have not verified or fully understood. Among the most productive tools (ChatGPT at 67 percent, Claude at 59 percent), a majority of users admitted to bot-shitting monthly. Forty percent of workers who blame AI when work fails show what psychologists call moral disengagement -- a gradual mental process of stopping accountability.

* **Who is bot-sitting more**: Heavy AI users report frequent botsitting more than light users, but tools sprawl is a bigger culprit. Workers using multiple AI tools are 35 percent more likely to report frequent botsitting. The "exhaustion multiplier" finding is striking: for every 10 percent more time spent feeding context, workers are 25 percent more likely to feel worn out. High-frequency bot-sitters are far more likely to be actively hunting for other jobs.

* **High AI achievers do things differently**: Workers and teams who report both productivity and quality gains do distinct things. High-achieving individuals still do more of their core work themselves rather than automating it all away. They botsit productively -- using the process as learning. Crucially, those who reinvest the 11 hours saved into new skills get real organizational dividends.

* **Cross-functional teammates drive AI adoption above all**: The report found one surprising cascade effect: when a leader uses AI, average employees adopt 2.4x more; a peer drives 3.2x adoption; and cross-functional peers drive 5.6x adoption. These are the workers who "survive contact with real work" -- they have to deal with the messy coordination that everyone else wishes away.

* **Transformative vs non-transformative organizations**: The 13 percent of companies where AI significantly improved organizational performance share patterns. They measure quality and time saved over vanity metrics, give employees visibility into their own AI usage (71 percent vs. 40 percent), review policies regularly (93 percent vs. 55 percent), explain policy rationale (91 percent vs. 57 percent), and formally reward AI skills in compensation (84 percent vs. 48 percent).

* **KPMG study on sophisticated collaboration**: A companion KPMG and UT Austin study of 1.4 million real workplace AI interactions found the highest-impact users are not better prompt engineers -- they treat AI as a reasoning partner, framing problems and iterating. These behaviors are teachable at scale.

The key takeaway: individual productivity gains do not automatically translate to organizational performance without intentional infrastructure built at three levels -- individuals, teams, and organizations. The companies getting real returns from AI are building that infrastructure deliberately, while the vast majority have simply handed workers new tools without addressing the messy coordination and quality assurance that follows. This episode is a call for leaders to stop treating AI strategy as vendor selection and start building the systems that make sophisticated collaboration scaleable.
