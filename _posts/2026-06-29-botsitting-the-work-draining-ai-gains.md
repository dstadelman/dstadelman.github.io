# Summary: Botsitting: The Work Draining AI Gains

[Nathaniel Whittemore - Botsitting: The Work Draining AI Gains](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/Botsitting-The-Work-Draining-AI-Gains-e3lapj5)

A recent episode of the AI Daily Brief dissects two major reports on the gap between individual AI productivity gains and organizational performance: Glean's Work AI Index 2026, which surveyed 6,000 digital workers, and a joint KPMG/University of Texas at Austin study analyzing 1.4 million workplace AI interactions. The core finding is striking -- while 87% of workers use AI and report saving 11 hours per week, only 13% say their organizations are performing significantly better as a result.

* **The bot-sitting phenomenon**: Workers spend 6.4 hours weekly on what the report calls "bot-sitting" -- feeding missing context to AI, checking outputs, debugging mistakes, and cleaning up confident but wrong answers. Of that time, 2.3 hours go to feeding context, 2.2 hours to supervising outputs, and 1.7 hours to debugging. Frequent bot-sitters (the top 50% of users) are 73% more likely to be actively job hunting.
* **Bot-shitting -- the cognitive surrender**: The report introduces a companion term: "bot-shitting" -- when fatigue from bot-sitting leads workers to stop checking AI outputs, stop interrogating sources, and stop feeling responsible for results. Heavy AI users are 3.4 times more likely to blame the tool when something goes wrong. As the report notes, it is "rarely a single bad decision but a slow surrender of agency one shortcut at a time."
* **The smarter-tool paradox**: Tools with the biggest productivity gains (ChatGPT at 67%, Claude at 59%) also produced users who admitted to bot-shitting most frequently -- 71% and 92% respectively. As models get better, the risk grows that users cannot verify outputs in domains they do not master.
* **What high achievers do differently**: High AI-achieving individuals still do more of their core work themselves rather than fully automating it, use bot-sitting productively as a learning mechanism, and reinvest saved time into new skills rather than more work. High-performing teams keep accountability on people, delegate coordination to AI, and treat AI adoption as spreading peer-to-peer -- with cross-functional adopters making teammates 5.6 times more likely to adopt.
* **Transformative organizations**: The 13% of companies seeing real organizational gains have radically different practices: measuring outcomes over vanity metrics (71% vs. 40% have workers see their own AI usage data), reviewing AI policy regularly (93% vs. 55%), and formalizing AI skill rewards (84% vs. 48%).
* **The agentic amplification argument**: The podcast host argues that the Work AI Index data was collected Dec 2025-Jan 2026, before fully agentic tools became widespread. Rather than making these findings less relevant, he contends that bot-sitting and bot-shitting would be even more pronounced with autonomous agents today.

This episode is valuable not just for naming the invisible labor consuming AI productivity gains but for mapping what separates organizational AI transformations from mere tool adoption. The gap isn't about workers not trying -- it's about the absence of the human infrastructure needed to translate individual efficiency into collective capability. Organizations that succeed treat AI like a reasoning partner, invest in people over vendor selection, and make governance a living system rather than a document on a shelf.
