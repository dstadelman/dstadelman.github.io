# Summary: AI Agents Are Moving Into the Real World

[The AI Daily Brief - AI Agents Are Moving Into the Real World](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-09-24)

Host Nathaniel Whittemore opens with Anthropic's claim that Claude discovered a CRISPR-like enzyme system in the company's new Bay Area wet lab, then covers Trump rebranding AI as "super intelligence" from the UN podium the same week Sam Altman and Dario Amodei briefed the UN Security Council on AI risk. But the main event is evidence piling up that personal agents are real: Meta's Muse holds the number one spot on the App Store and moves onto Ray-Ban glasses and a new always-on "Charm" device, while Grok Bot lands inside Teslas -- all as skeptics like Ben Thompson insist consumers simply don't want to be productive.

* **Claude finds a CRISPR-like enzyme system:** Less than a week after confirming its Bay Area wet lab (BSL-1/2, with humans doing all the bench work), Anthropic says roughly 950-1,000 Claude agents, over 21 hours and about 210 million tokens (roughly $10,000 of compute), found a previously uncharacterized enzyme system in jumbo bacteriophages -- dubbed ART, array-associated reverse transcriptases -- with a long array of repeated DNA reminiscent of a CRISPR array, admitting the function and significance are still unknown.
* **Amodei frames it as a pattern:** The CEO, a biophysics PhD, promoted the discovery on X, citing AI math climbing from high-school level in 2023 to competition level in 2024 to solving open problems by late 2026, and argued AI for biology is on the same exponential -- the wet lab exists to confirm results physically, while the end goal of Claude autonomously controlling lab equipment is still distant.
* **Scientists call it real, fast, and very preliminary:** The consensus take is that finding a weird cluster of genes and repeats is the easy part -- the hard question is what the system actually does -- and at least one X critic read the timing near Anthropic's anticipated IPO as hype; Amodei separately conceded that a Stanford team previously described a similar but distinct system.
* **Trump rebrands AI from the UN podium:** In his Sept. 22 General Assembly speech, the president said the word "artificial" makes AI "sound fake" and declared that US documents would henceforth call it "super intelligence," hoping the world follows -- while the US "totally rejects any attempt to construct a globalist scheme to control" it -- generating instant memes, from Replit CEO Amjad Masad to a chorus of safety advocates.
* **The "globalist scheme" has a source:** Ahead of the UNGA, roughly 20 countries led by Finland (with Norway) adopted a "call for control of frontier AI models" calling for mandatory independent safety testing, common standards, and exploring an international AI institution that can convene states when capability thresholds are crossed -- a proposal that notably lacks both US and Chinese support, and which UN Secretary General Antonio Guterres welcomed.
* **The White House doubles down:** White House science advisor Michael Kratsios told the Security Council that a rapidly advancing intelligence frontier "is not a reason to pause its further development or to constrain it with new global governance structures," concluding that a prosperous future "will be secured by sovereign nations that adopt superintelligence, responsible companies that build it, and free people who refuse to be ruled by fear."
* **Altman charts the middle path:** Speaking live from New York, the OpenAI CEO framed AI as both the clearest danger and the biggest opportunity of the coming decades, organizing his pitch around two risks -- loss of control as models outgrow their developers' understanding, and concentration of power, warning that a company or country that believes only it can be trusted with the technology "can use that belief to justify almost anything else" -- adding that whether catastrophe risk is 10% or 1%, "none of these levels are remotely acceptable."
* **Amodei's playbook:** Joining by video link, the Anthropic CEO offered a step-by-step plan -- simple treaties with broad consensus such as a ban on using AI to develop biological weapons, global evaluation and verification systems so states can check each other's commitments, and common standards for testing loss-of-control and misuse risks plus a global notification system -- closing with the warning that, "if managed poorly, I even believe that AI could be a risk to humanity as a whole."
* **The skeptics are loud:** Stratechery's Ben Thompson went viral repeating that consumers don't want to be productive -- "I'm enjoying myself complaining right now, okay? Would you just let me be and live my life?" -- and a widely shared X post this week argued AI assistants don't abstract decisions, they accelerate them, so instead of one decision a day you get ten, and "nobody wants to realize their potential. People want to hang out with friends and complain," a sentiment even Kurt Vonnegut voiced in a 2005 interview about enjoying the errand of buying a single envelope because, as he put it, "we're dancing animals."
* **The counterpoint is concrete:** Investor Amy Wu Martin says painting all consumers with one brush is reductive, fintech creator Simon Taylor argues people want to delegate "schlep work" like booking a daughter's cycling class or coordinating a dinner time across calendars (and Muse and Instinct users "will tell you it's magic"), investor Jill Gunter points to "two billion mothers in the world," and Madhu Guru, who works on AI at Meta, notes that browsing clothes can be entertainment while hiring a roofer is miserable -- the latent demand to remove friction is immense.
* **A New York Times review swings the debate:** Eli Tan's Sept. 22 piece "I Gave My Life Over to Meta's A.I. Agent and Was Blown Away" found Muse handling a dental insurance call, paying a parking ticket from a photo, and turning $45 in saved money into a Facebook Marketplace haggle for Heath Ceramics bowls -- "the most useful AI app I had ever used" -- leaving him skeptical less about usefulness than about whether enough people will accept the privacy implications of handing over that much data.
* **Meta goes all in:** Muse held the number one App Store spot as the episode was recorded, Meta Chief AI Officer Alexandr Wang was in a partnership frenzy (Walmart, DICK'S Sporting Goods, Gap, Sephora, Box, and more), and new features included Mac computer use and a real-time video avatar that talks to you while working in the background.
* **Meta Connect 2026 is the Muse show:** At Wednesday's event, Meta brought Muse to Ray-Ban glasses for hands-free all-day use, debuted its first audio-only, camera-free Ray-Ban, and refreshed the flagship line -- a far stronger Connect than the anti-climaxes of past years, since much of the hardware roadmap is explicitly built to move the agent into physical life.
* **The "one more thing":** Zuckerberg closed with Muse Charm, a pocket-sized always-on device (about the size of a stopwatch or AirPods case) with a small colored screen that takes voice instructions without unlocking a phone -- "by far the fastest way to talk to your Muse," in his words -- still in active development but with Meta hoping to ship it in time for the holidays.
* **The mood has actually shifted:** Six months ago most observers would have reflexively dismissed an always-on personal agent from Meta, but early reactions are genuinely interested -- one commentator sketched the two futures (delegate your time-eaters while keeping your human creativity, versus becoming obsessed with a little character that decides where you eat and what you buy "and you won't even notice because it will feel like help") -- and Steve Howe, head of research at Silicon Data, wrote, "I think this may finally have changed," as even a Google DeepMind researcher came across as optimistic.
* **Grok Bot moves into Teslas:** xAI's agent launched in the car on Sept. 22, checking email, managing calendars, and running multi-step errands hands-free via "connectors" -- Devin Olsen ordered a Starbucks while Full Self-Driving drove, and Tesla fan Sawyer Merritt showed it off live -- though for now it's gated to the $300-a-month SuperGrok Heavy tier, even as more ambitious builders like Alex Finn already plan bigger uses.
* **But trust the numbers carefully:** X commentator Riley Brown notes it's hard to trust a number-one ranking when a company with Meta's size and distribution can push an app to the top, and his best tell comes from a poker table where everyone was both convinced AI would end civilization within a decade and genuinely using Muse -- "you don't have to ask it to do stuff, you just connect it and it starts working for you."
* **Two questions to watch:** How much this bridge into the physical world -- glasses, a pocket Charm device, the car -- will actually matter for adoption, and whether the secret sauce is a shift from agents as a reactive experience (you have to ask) to a proactive one that brings value to you without your asking.

The through-line is that the "nobody wants personal agents" debate finally has evidence, and the evidence so far is that people who actually try these things keep coming away more impressed than they expected -- with the open question no longer whether agents can do the schlep, but whether enough people will feel comfortable trusting them with so much of their lives.
