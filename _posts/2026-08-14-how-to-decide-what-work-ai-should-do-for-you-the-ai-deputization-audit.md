# Summary: How to Decide What Work AI Should Do for You: The AI Deputization Audit

[AI Daily Brief - How to Decide What Work AI Should Do for You: The AI Deputization Audit](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48)

The episode pairs a news roundup -- Gemini 3.7 Flash, OpenAI's speed-focused Ultrafast mode, an AlphaSense cost study, and OpenAI's CRO turnover -- with a practical activity: the AI Deputization Audit, a score-and-tier process for figuring out which parts of your work AI should actually take on. The unifying frame is that AI's bottleneck has shifted from model capability to access to context, and two features launched this week -- GrokBot's teach-a-task and ChatGPT's Computer History -- make that shift concrete by letting an agent learn how you work. Once the AI can see your work, the remaining question is a decision framework, not a capability gap.

* **The bottleneck is context, not capability**: The core claim is that just because a model can do something does not mean it has the information and context to do it well for you specifically. The new feature space is all about closing that gap: GrokBot adds a "teach a task" mode where you record yourself doing a browser workflow and it turns the demonstration into a reusable skill [xAI docs](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/faq), and OpenAI ships Computer History, which lets ChatGPT "learn from everything you do on your computer" [Futurism - Aug 2026](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/new-chatgpt-feature-collects-every-keystroke-you-make).

* **Google's Gemini 3.7 Flash is built for speed, not the frontier**: At 340 tokens per second it is more than twice as fast as GPT-5.6 Luna and even edges Nvidia's Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, with a solid jump on the DeepSWE coding benchmark (48.6 to 65.3) over 3.6 Flash and a halved price. But on the Artificial Analysis cost run it still lands at about 40 cents per task -- roughly the same as MuSpark, pricier than Nemotron 3 Ultra and GLM 5.2, and around eight times the ultra-cheap tier -- putting it in an uncomfortable cost-per-intelligence middle ground [Artificial Analysis - Aug 2026](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/gemini-3-7-time-frontier).

* **The counter-case: for interactive coding, speed changes the math**: Vercel's Brandon Galang argues 3.7 Flash sits on the Pareto frontier once you account for GPT-5.6 Luna being a much smaller model and his team's production workloads, while analyst Max Weinbach notes that for partially-synchronous coding -- where you sit and interact with the agent's output rather than fire-and-forget -- the speed boost can justify a slightly higher price even at a below-frontier performance level. The same efficiency gains also make it a meaningful upgrade for Google's personal agent, Gemini Spark.

* **The "model race" is splitting into several races**: NLW's takeaway is that there is no longer a single frontier race -- there are also races for distribution, for harnesses, and for revenue -- and that within models alone, Google is explicitly betting that speed is a dimension buyers will pay attention to.

* **Cheaper Chinese models do not automatically mean higher savings**: An AlphaSense study ran nine models through 245 real-world financial-analysis tasks built from earnings calls, filings, and news, scoring for factual accuracy and multi-analyst nuance. GPT-5.6 Sol finished about 13% cheaper than Kimi K3 with roughly 20% higher quality; Google's Gemma 4 and Thinking Machines' Inkling matched GLM 5.2's quality at under a fifth the cost; and Opus 5 was the outlier, delivering lower quality than Opus 4.8 at more than five times the price [The Information - Aug 2026](https://www.theinformation.com/newsletters/applied-ai/anthropic-models-can-cheaper-use-chinese-ones-study-finds) [Thinking Machines - Inkling](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/introducing-inkling/). As AlphaSense CEO Jack Kokko put it, some of the higher-priced models actually ended up costing less because they used tokens more efficiently.

* **OpenAI answers on speed with Ultrafast mode**: A new API service tier runs GPT-5.6 Sol at up to 14 times the standard pace -- up to 750 output tokens per second -- powered by Cerebras, and is available first to a select group of customers, pitched at latency-sensitive work like incident response, financial research, real-time voice, and commerce, with no pricing disclosed [OpenAI - Aug 13, 2026](https://openai.com/index/previewing-ultrafast/).

* **OpenAI's chief revenue officer exits after about nine months**: Denise Dresser, the Salesforce veteran and former Slack CEO brought in to ease investor concerns during a period when OpenAI's growth was being questioned, is leaving, to be replaced by Dali Rajic, the former president and COO of Wiz [CNBC - Aug 13, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/13/openai-denise-dresser-executive-exits.html) [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/dali-rajic-chief-revenue-officer/). It lands days after Brad Lightcap's departure and following Fidji Simo's July step-down from the AGI-deployment role over a medical leave [TechCrunch - Jul 9, 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/09/fidji-simo-steps-down-from-openais-no-2-role/), with Axios sourcing that President Greg Brockman has been standing up his own team of leaders; the episode flags the pattern as a pre-IPO problem, though the listing is now pushed to next year.

* **The audit, step one -- inventory your recurring processes**: Email triage, weekly status reports, meeting prep, research briefs, CRM or pipeline hygiene, content repurposing, scheduling and travel, vendor-portal chores, inbound lead qualification, and metrics pulls. The focus is on the work you do day-in, day-out, because that is where a "deputize it to AI" decision actually pays.

* **Step two -- score each process on five dimensions (0 to 2 each)**: Is it worth automating (how often, and how much time it eats); is it teachable (can you demo it in a ten-minute screen share); is the output checkable (does verifying it take less time than producing it); what are the stakes if the AI gets it wrong and nobody catches it; and how essential is it that it specifically be you (would someone else's output be just as good).

* **Step three -- tier by the total (0 to 10)**: Scores of 8 to 10 are "depute" candidates -- hand it over, spot-check, and iterate; 0 to 3 are "defend" -- keep that work to yourself because a mistake is expensive or the person on the other end expects you specifically; and 4 to 7 are "duet" -- AI does part of it, you stay heavily involved. Most knowledge work today sits in that duet band, and the open question is whether better tools shift enough of it into the depute tier over time.

* **Step four -- name the specific blocker**: For the duet-and-defend items, write down exactly what is stopping you from handing it off. Some blockers dissolve with these new tools -- work trapped in legacy software with no API (computer-use agents that click the same screens you do), or processes you know how to do but struggle to write down (now you can show rather than tell). Others survive -- work requiring taste or judgment, mistakes that are costly or irreversible, and tasks that depend on human relationships -- and if the blocker is privacy or security, recording your screen can actually introduce a new one.

* **Computer History is Windows Recall, except now people volunteer it**: Microsoft's 2024 screenshot-every-few-seconds feature was branded a privacy nightmare and had to be reworked; yet the pattern of a machine quietly learning your activity is now what users actively want. Two things changed: the value proposition shifted from "find something you've seen before on your PC" to "an agent that actually does your work," and the technical design matters -- OpenAI's Ari Weinstein describes Computer History as recording interaction events rather than taking screenshots or audio, with incognito and private-mode content excluded by default [Futurism - Aug 2026](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/new-chatgpt-feature-collects-every-keystroke-you-make).

* **The day-two reality of GrokBot**: Superintelligent's Nufar Gaspar says it is a strong fit for people who have not yet built full agent systems but hits ceilings for advanced users -- no control over which folder holds the relevant context, and no model choice. Meanwhile a plumber, John O'Neill, went from zero to automated dispatch and office chores in about a day with no engineers on payroll, and a recurring pattern of spinning up one bot per task behind a chief-of-staff coordinator is emerging as early best practice -- with the universal starter job being an inbox/Slack tracker plus a morning brief, now set up in about a minute.

**Primary takeaway**: The AI Deputization Audit is the durable artifact -- a repeatable, five-criterion score-and-tier framework that turns "should I let AI do this" into something you can actually decide, and it is the reason the context tooling this week (GrokBot's teach-a-task, ChatGPT's Computer History) finally has a use case that feels like leverage rather than surveillance. The honest constraint is that the biggest barrier is not model quality but carving out time to relearn how you work -- so start with the 8-to-10 tasks, name the blockers on everything else, and watch the tools, not the capability gap, close the distance.
