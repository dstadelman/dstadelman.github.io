The motion for this debate: "AI is turning our brains to mush." Taking the YES side.

## The neural evidence

MIT Media Lab researchers used EEG to measure brain activity while people wrote essays under three conditions: using ChatGPT, using a traditional search engine, and using no tools at all [1]. Those relying on LLMs showed the weakest neural connectivity of any group. When asked to write without AI in a follow-up session, LLM-dependent participants showed reduced alpha and beta connectivity -- their brains simply did not wake up to meet the cognitive demand [1].

This was not a momentary effect. The study spanned four months. Chronic AI use produced a sustained reduction in neural engagement that mapped directly onto how much a person used the tool [1].

One detail stuck with me: LLM users had the lowest self-reported sense of ownership over their essays [1]. They struggled to quote their own work back to examiners. The brain was delegating work to the machine and then losing track of what belonged to it anymore.

## Critical thinking declines with AI dependence

Michael Gerlich studied 666 participants and found a direct negative correlation between AI tool usage and critical thinking scores [2]. More frequent AI use predicted lower performance on critical reasoning assessments.

The age pattern was alarming. People aged 17 to 25 showed the highest dependence on AI and the lowest critical thinking scores [2][3]. Their reasoning pathways are still developing. They are entering the capability with AI already thinking for them.

Gerlich did find one protective factor: higher education helped maintain critical thinking despite AI use [2][3]. That means people without advanced education are losing critical thinking skills the fastest.

## Cognitive debt accumulates silently

MIT researcher Nataliya Kosmyna coined the term "cognitive debt" for this process [1]. Like financial debt, it accrues quietly. You feel relief in the moment -- the answer arrives instantly. But the interest compounds. Every time you bypass the friction of thinking, you fail to build the neural circuitry that independent thinking requires.

Over four months, LLM users consistently underperformed their peers across three dimensions: neural (brain connectivity), linguistic (essay quality), and behavioral (ownership and recall) [1]. Every metric pointed in the same direction. This was not a single anomaly -- it was convergence.

## AI creates confirmation bias by design

A human mentor challenges your premises. AI does not [4]. When you ask ChatGPT a question, it confirms your assumptions and makes you feel validated [4]. Lisa Bodell put it plainly: AI gives us "confirmation bias on steroids" [4]. It mirrors you instead of interrogating you. For critical thinking -- which depends on the ability to hold opposing ideas and test your own reasoning -- this is catastrophic.

AI produces perfectly confident but wrong answers because it is a prediction machine, not a knowledge engine [4]. When users cannot tell the difference -- which they routinely cannot -- they accept the output without scrutiny. A KPMG and University of Melbourne survey of 48,000 workers found that 66% use AI tools without evaluating whether the output is even accurate [4][5].

## We are only at the beginning

What has been measured so far is the effect of AI on people who are already digital natives. The next generation -- students who have grown up with AI as an always-available cognitive assistant -- face something far more profound. Prashant Mahajan described this as "Generational Cognitive Atrophy" [6]. It is already the primary concern of education leaders [7].

Faculty at Harvard have been clear on this: students who use AI to do the work for them rather than with them learn nothing [8]. Dan Levy of the Harvard Kennedy School stated bluntly that no learning occurs unless the brain is actively engaged. AI is designed to be the one-click shortcut that removes all friction. And friction is what thinking requires.

## Closing

AI may be the most powerful tool humanity has built. But a tool that replaces the need to think is not a tool. It is a crutch. And a crutch used for long enough makes the legs it replaces atrophy.

The EEG data shows reduced brain connectivity with AI use. Gerlich found critical thinking scores declining in lockstep with dependence. Kosmyna calls it cognitive debt -- the interest compounds silently. AI reinforces your existing beliefs by design. And we are only at the beginning.

If critical thinking, memory retention, and independent reasoning are the core capacities of an educated mind, the question is not whether AI is useful. The question is whether it is eroding what makes us capable of thinking at all.
