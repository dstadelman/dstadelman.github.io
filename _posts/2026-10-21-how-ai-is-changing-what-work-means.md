# Summary: How AI Is Changing What Work Means

[AI Daily Brief - How AI Is Changing What Work Means](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-AI-Is-Changing-What-Work-Means-e3qsa48)

The episode's headline story sounds like clickbait -- a financial research firm turned itself into a bio-lab and went after scorpion venom -- but host Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW) argues it is the clearest illustration yet of what comes next for AI at work. Citrini Research, a thematic equity and global macro shop, couldn't get a straight answer from experts about whether AI drug discovery was real, so it ran the experiment itself with Anthropic's help for under a couple hundred thousand dollars. The point is not that AI made an antivenom; it is that a new category of "orthogonal work" is now economically viable. Around that spine: Meta's Muse hits 10 million US downloads, Anthropic's leaked IPO prospectus hands critics an $80 billion number that isn't really a number, and OpenAI buys a drug-development coordination startup.

* **Citrini Therapeutics**: A financial research firm -- not a biotech -- set out to test AI's real capability in biology after concluding life sciences was "a secular story at cyclical lows" and that surveying experts only produced the full range from "AI has never made an important drug" to "AI will cure all diseases in five years." Their write-up: "We weren't going to get a clear answer by simply asking. We needed to try it for ourselves. So we did."
* **The experiment**: Starting from a 35-page prompt Anthropic had already tested in a prior study, Citrini ran roughly 300 Claude sub-agents for 64 hours, generating about 318,000 protein designs and shortlisting candidates that were synthesized and independently tested by contract labs Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience. Seventeen designs confirmed as binders against four of the five targeted scorpion neurotoxins.
* **The honest caveat**: None of the proteins has been shown to neutralize the venom -- binding is not the same as stopping the toxin. Anthropic's own technical report is blunt that no in vitro or in vivo neutralization testing was done, and the strongest binders (Cn2-B1, Cn2-B2) only compete with an antibody known to neutralize in mice, which is suggestive, not proof.
* **The skeptic's hit**: One widely shared critique noted that proteins binding scorpion toxins were demonstrated in the literature 15-plus years ago and that the thing that actually matters was buried in "10,000 words of Claude prose." The Verge quoted Technical University of Denmark researchers calling the excitement "premature" and the results "far from a medicine and hyped."
* **Why scorpion venom at all**: Scorpions sting about 1.2 million people a year and kill roughly 3,200, mostly children in regions with thin medical infrastructure. Anthropic's argument is that these treatments are expensive to develop and unlikely to ever earn their development cost back -- exactly the class of neglected problem a step-change in discovery cost could rescue.
* **The DeepMind read**: Google DeepMind's director of AGI economics Alex Imas said Citrini got everyone talking about AI for science while also illustrating the real constraint -- "huge speed-ups in hypothesis generation and computation, but also the bottlenecks: verification and testing." Knowing which half is which is how you know where to invest.
* **The chart that hurt**: Former OpenAI researcher Will DePue put two of Citrini's charts side by side: 64 hours to dry-lab design, 39 days to lab results, four to seven years to release. "God, these two charts really hurt my soul." Citrini's own conclusion: DNA still had to be synthesized, proteins produced and purified, toxins obtained, controls established -- "our digital invention was now set to create work for human scientists."
* **OpenAI buys Orchestra Bio**: Announced Tuesday, terms undisclosed; the four-year-old startup raised about $20 million and builds agents that streamline drug-development workflows -- monitoring compound manufacturing, tracking trials -- rather than proposing new compounds. The team folds into GPT-Rosalind, OpenAI's life-sciences model, which just moved from April research preview to full commercial product alongside the Rosalind Workbench agent suite.
* **Tools, not a pharma company**: OpenAI framed the deal as supporting existing industry, citing its Novo Nordisk partnership and Moderna, where "AI is helping teams translate insights from complex data into experimental workflows." This is explicitly not OpenAI building its own biotech firm.
* **Anthropic's different bet**: Per Reuters, Anthropic quietly stood up its own physical wet lab in the San Francisco Bay Area. Head of life sciences Eric Kauderer-Abrams confirmed it: "We believe that to do biology, the final test is still and will be for a while in real lab work." Reporting showed a limited remit, with the company stopping short of clinical trials.
* **Two labs, two models of the AI business**: One lab sells intelligence to the industry; the other is starting to use intelligence to do the work itself. That feeds the unverified reports that Anthropic leadership believes it will ultimately be the only private company left -- though Citrini's experiment shows how many people, steps, and institutions sit between an AI-designed molecule and a real product.
* **Would curing cancer fix AI's image problem?**: The popular theory says disease cures are the only way to flip public sentiment. NLW is skeptical: AI-accelerated discovery is likelier to become the boring norm than to land as one miracle compound, and as investor Will Manidis noted, if the industry thinks curing cancer is enough, it should look at opinion polling on big pharma. His conclusion: there is no magic bullet, so labs should work on what they think has the biggest impact regardless of headlines.
* **The real thesis -- orthogonal work**: Citrini was clear it is not becoming a biolab; it ran a wet-lab experiment because it was the cheapest way to answer an investment question. Expect far more of this: firms and people doing things they previously could never justify, purely because it makes their core work better. That is the opportunity side of the efficiency-versus-opportunity divide.
* **Muse races to 10 million**: Per Sensor Tower, Meta's Muse hit 10.2 million US downloads (10.9 million worldwide) in its first 40 days since its September 8 launch -- only the 12th app to do that since tracking began in 2015. The other 11 are almost entirely mobile smash hits like Pokemon Go, Fortnite, and PUBG; the only non-gaming comparables are Disney+ and Meta's own Threads. Notably, ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini never grew this fast as mobile apps because each launched on the web first.
* **The growth is bought as much as earned**: Sensor Tower says Meta allocated 39% of its total October US advertising spend to Muse, up from 23% in September, making Muse the 21st-largest US brand by impressions for the month.
* **Retention beats the chatbots, and the critics benchmark wrong**: 30% retention at seven days and 23% at 14, versus roughly 19% and 16% averages for ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok, against about 5% at 30 days for the average iOS app. The critical takes compare Muse to Instagram, Facebook, and Google -- some of the stickiest consumer apps ever built. Daily active users hit 2.5 million on October 16, up 15% week over week.
* **Agents are stuck in a retention no-man's-land**: As Signal argued on X, consumer apps retain through desire (games, social) or dependency (email, banking), and personal AI agents occupy a weird third space -- not emotionally rewarding enough to be entertainment, not reliable enough to be infrastructure. Outside pure research tasks, one failure is often enough to lose the user.
* **Muse's GhostShares leak**: Security firm Claroc found that Meta built share URLs with the conversation title in the subdomain under cf.metaaiusercontent.com, so a single passive-DNS search enumerated 6,800 share links. Opening a few hundred turned up a small-business owner's loan application, a real estate investor's internal performance report, a national marketing company's internal revenue review, and a to-do list containing a bank fraud alert with a partial account number. Meta paused the feature; Claroc had reported it through the bug bounty program on October 8.
* **Gemini finally gets context in your car**: After about a year as a mostly voice remote for maps and stereo, the Android Auto Gemini assistant can now reach the rest of your Google services -- asking whether you'll miss your flight pulls details from your inbox while checking Maps traffic, and it can route you to a coffee shop from a photo you took there last week. NLW is genuinely torn on whether this matters, since the phone with the same capability is usually already on the console. Tesla has integrated Grok -- including Grok Bot for multistep tasks -- into its vehicles, and everyone else will likely follow.
* **Anthropic's $80 billion loss, decoded**: The Wall Street Journal obtained the updated draft prospectus with audited first-half financials, and the press fixated on an $80.7 billion net loss and up to $750 billion in compute spending. About $72 billion of the loss is an on-paper fair-value charge on convertible notes and warrants that grows as the valuation climbs, leaving roughly $8.7 billion as the real operating loss.
* **The Farley rebuttal**: Financial podcaster Jack Farley: "this would be worrisome if the $80 billion accounting loss by Anthropic were economically real in any way. Good news, it isn't." When a company issues convertible notes and warrants and its market cap 1000Xs, the on-paper loss from being short calls is massive, not fundamental.
* **The $750 billion number is really $450 billion over a decade**: Firm, uncancelable compute commitments sit around $450 billion due over seven to ten years -- high but far more tenable than the headline. There may also be double-ordering, with soft commitments to neoclouds in hopes they actually finish the facilities. The Journal's line: "Anthropic needs more computing power than the market can provide."
* **Booked revenue looks smaller than the run rate**: Audited first-half revenue of $16.3 billion sits below the $65 billion annual run rate Anthropic claimed through July -- both can be true, but booked numbers land lower than expected. The $16.8 billion added in Q3 (45% quarter-over-quarter growth) disclosed at last week's investor day is unaudited and won't make the prospectus.
* **Nobody was actually surprised**: The leak mostly confirmed pre-existing biases -- the $80 billion figure gives critics fresh podcast material, but no one imagined Anthropic hadn't issued a mountain of options or wouldn't spend north of half a trillion on compute. Like the SpaceX IPO, this offering will be priced on what people believe about the next decade, not the last few quarters.
* **Investors nudging the valuation down**: Reuters reports some investors pushing back on the rumored $2 trillion valuation and signaling comfort nearer $1.5 trillion -- worth noting both numbers come from investors, with nothing attributed to Anthropic or its bankers.
* **OpenAI is racing to close first**: Sources tell Reuters OpenAI is trying to wrap a $30 billion raise at a $1.4 trillion valuation within weeks, ahead of the Anthropic IPO and whatever market conditions it brings, while capitalizing on growth after the release of GPT-6 Astra. Even for this sector, that is an unusually fast deal.

The through-line is that AI's next move at work isn't faster email -- it's changed scope. Citrini didn't become a biotech company; it did a biology experiment because the cost of doing something outside its lane collapsed enough to make it the cheapest way to answer its own investment question. That's the shift to watch: not efficiency within a job, but whole categories of orthogonal work becoming justifiable, with all the physical-world inertia -- 64 hours of design, four to seven years to release -- still fully intact.
