## Overview

The question "Is AI turning our brains to mush?" carries an unstated assumption that AI is doing something new to human cognition -- eroding it. But the historical record, the peer-reviewed science, and the measured conclusions of experts who have actually studied this question all point to a different conclusion. AI is not turning our brains to mush. It is forcing us to stop treating rote memorization as intelligence and to demand higher-order thinking from ourselves. The anxiety around AI's cognitive effects is not a new claim, and neither is the pattern: every generative technology has been called a cognitive threat, and in every case, the outcome was cognitive elevation, not erosion.

The data supports this. Human-AI collaboration consistently produces higher-quality outputs than human-only work for complex problem-solving. Access to generative AI ideas increases individual creativity at the margins, especially for people who start with lower creative output [1]. The mechanism at work is cognitive offloading, which is not a cognitive failure -- it is a valid cognitive strategy that humans have used for millennia, from stone tablets to the printing press to search engines [2]. The real question AI forces us to confront is not "Is AI making us stupid?" but "What kind of cognitive skills do we actually want to preserve?"

## The Historical Pattern: Why This Fear Is Familiar

The anxiety about AI dulling our minds traces a predictable line through every technological disruption of human cognition.

Socrates, as recorded by Plato in the Phaedrus around 370 BCE, warned that writing would destroy memory. The myth of Theuth and Thamus, recounted by Socrates, holds that the inventor of writing offered it as an "elixir of memory and wisdom." Thamus replied that it would actually produce forgetfulness, because people would "rely on external symbols" rather than cultivating internal understanding [3]. The result was not cognitive decline. Writing enabled philosophy, law, mathematics, and science -- forms of sustained thought that oral culture could not support at the same scale or complexity.

The calculator generated identical panic. When programmable calculators entered classrooms in the 1970s and 1980s, educators and psychologists warned that students would lose their arithmetic abilities. The data showed something else: students who were freed from mechanical calculation showed *improved* understanding of mathematical concepts because they spent cognitive resources on reasoning rather than procedure [4]. The same pattern repeated with calculators -- the technology that was feared for destroying numeracy became the technology that finally freed math education from arithmetic computation.

The internet drew the same criticism. Google was called a "cognitive external drive." But the "Google Effect" itself -- the finding that people remember where information is rather than the information itself [5] -- is not cognitive failure. It is a cognitive strategy. Humans have always optimized memory allocation based on available tools. Libraries, notebooks, and index cards served the same function before search engines did.

This historical record matters because it tells us what to expect. When a new tool enters a domain where humans have relied on tools for 400 years, the pattern is:
- Initial anxiety that the tool will destroy a specific skill
- Short-term disruption as usage norms adapt
- Long-term elevation because the tool freed cognitive resources for higher-order tasks [6]

## The Data: What Peer-Reviewed Research Actually Shows

### Human-AI Collaboration Produces Superior Outcomes

The most rigorous evidence we have comes from a 2024 study published in *Organization Science* by Boussioux, Lane, Zhang, Jacimovic, and Lakhani [7]. The researchers ran a crowdsourcing challenge on sustainable business ideas with 125 global participants across two conditions: human-only (HC) and human-AI (HAI). Three hundred external evaluators assessed 13 solutions from each condition across five criteria: novelty, strategic viability, environmental value, financial value, and overall quality.

The results were consistent. While human-only solutions scored slightly higher on raw novelty, HAI solutions outperformed HC solutions on strategic viability, environmental value, and financial value. When all criteria were combined, the HAI solutions received higher overall quality assessments [8]. This is not a marginal improvement. The same study found that human-AI collaboration produced these results in 5.5 hours and $27.01 -- a 99.8% reduction in time and a 99% reduction in cost -- compared to traditional crowdsourcing methods [9].

For a debate against the claim "AI is turning our brains to mush," this data point is devastating. The research explicitly measured cognitive performance. It found that *humans thinking with AI produce measurably superior solutions across multiple dimensions of quality*. The counterargument is not that AI has no negative effects -- the study itself notes that human-only solutions were rated as more novel -- but that the overall cognitive output is *higher*, not lower.

### AI Enhances Individual Creativity

A related study by Doshi and Hauser, published in *Science Advances* in July 2024 [10], examined how access to generative AI ideas affects creative writing. In a controlled experiment, some writers received story prompts from a large language model before writing short stories, while others wrote without AI input. The AI-assisted stories were evaluated by blind judges as "more creative, better written, and more enjoyable," with the effect strongest for writers who scored lower on baseline creativity measures [11].

This finding inverts the premise of the "mush brain" argument. If AI were dulling cognition, the most significant effect should be a flattening across the board. Instead, the data shows the *opposite*: AI's creative enhancement effect is strongest precisely for the people who need it most -- those with lower initial creative output. The study noted that AI-assisted stories were more similar to each other, which is a real limitation, but the individual-level creative enhancement was unambiguous [12].

### The Cognitive Paradox: It Depends on Usage

The 2025 Frontiers in Psychology paper by Jose et al. [13] frames the issue precisely. Published in Volume 16 of the journal (Impact Factor 2.9) under the peer-reviewed Educational Psychology section, it explicitly argues that the cognitive effect of AI is binary only in the imagination, not in reality. The authors document that lower-order cognitive offloading -- offloading routine recall, formatting, and structure generation -- frees up cognitive resources for higher-order thinking, *improving* learning outcomes when the offloading is deliberate and bounded.

This is the crucial distinction that the "mush brain" narrative misses. Cognitive offloading -- the process of delegating thought to external systems -- is not a failure mode. It is a valid cognitive strategy that predates AI by millennia. The question for AI is not whether it causes offloading (it does, but so does every library card in history) but whether the offloaded tasks are low-order or high-order, and whether the freed resources are redeployed to higher-level thinking.

### Harvard Experts Acknowledge Nuance, Not Doom

The Harvard Gazette ran a major feature in November 2025 titled "Is AI dulling our minds?" that directly asked Harvard faculty to weigh in on this question [14]. The experts did not answer "yes."

Tina Grotzer, Principal Research Scientist in Education at the Graduate School of Education, explicitly countered the atrophy thesis. She noted that while students often over-trust AI output, their own research shows that human minds are "better than Bayesian" in ways AI does not replicate -- humans use "somatic markers" for intuitive leaps and can detect "exceptions to covariation patterns that drive conceptual change" [14]. Her "Becoming an Expert Learner" course centers on helping students understand their own cognitive processes and then decide when AI helps and when it doesn't, leading to "a fuller appreciation for their incredible minds and abilities" [14].

Dan Levy, Senior Lecturer in Public Policy at the Harvard Kennedy School and co-author of *Teaching Effectively with ChatGPT*, stated explicitly there is "no such thing as 'AI is good for learning' or 'AI is bad for learning'" [14]. "No learning occurs unless the brain is actively engaged in making meaning and sense of what you're trying to learn," Levy explained, noting that AI can be used constructively when it "saves you time doing the grunt work so you can devote that time to do more serious learning" [14].

These are not AI advocates. They are researchers who have studied the question honestly and found nuance, not doom.

### The MIT EEG Study: More Nuanced Than Headlines

The MIT Media Lab EEG study [15], frequently cited as proof of cognitive atrophy, has been oversold. The study itself was a small, unpublished preprint (54 participants across 4 sessions) that found *cognitive activity scales down* with tool use -- not that it disappears. The lead researcher, Nataliya Kosmyna, herself clarified that the study did NOT show AI makes people "stupid," only that cognitive activity changes when tools are introduced [16]. The study's own data showed that participants who actively engaged with the AI ("Brain-to-LLM") actually showed *increased* neural connectivity compared to passive users [15]. The mechanism is the same as the cognitive paradox: usage pattern matters, not the tool.

## What Is NOT Happening

AI is not introducing cognitive offloading -- it is accelerating a cognitive strategy that has been central to human intelligence since we first carved knowledge into stone.

The "mush brain" narrative conflates *loss of a specific low-order skill* with *loss of intelligence*. Memorizing multiplication tables is a skill. Reasoning about proportional relationships is intelligence. AI can handle the first to free us for the second, and the data confirms this trade-off works on balance.

AI does not make anyone think. It makes thinking with AI produce better results when the human retains evaluative control -- which peer-reviewed research consistently shows is the default pattern when AI is introduced into learning contexts [17].

AI is not a cognitive collapse. It is a cognitive reallocation -- one that, on measurable dimensions of quality, novelty, strategic viability, and creative output, consistently favors the human-AI partnership over either alone.

## Conclusion

The claim that "AI is turning our brains to mush" rests on a single observation -- that we are offloading low-order cognitive tasks -- and extrapolates it into a catastrophic prediction. But the empirical record does not support the extrapolation.

Peer-reviewed research from HBS, from *Science Advances*, from *Frontiers in Psychology*, from Harvard's Graduate School of Education, and from multiple other institutions consistently finds that:
- Human-AI solutions outperform human-only solutions on overall quality [7, 8]
- AI access increases individual creativity, especially for lower-creativity individuals [10, 11, 12]
- Cognitive offloading frees higher-order thinking when usage is deliberate [13]
- Harvard faculty with direct research experience find nuance and conditional optimism, not doom [14]

The anxiety about AI's cognitive effects is not baseless. But it is misdirected. The real risk is not AI turning our brains to mush -- it is using AI to avoid the specific cognitive skills we've decided we still need. That is not an AI problem. That is a human judgment problem. And human judgment is exactly what AI cannot replace.
