# The Legal AI Arms Race: Tools, Layoffs, and the Playbook for Legal Departments

The legal tech landscape is undergoing its most dramatic transformation since the digitization of document management — and it's not happening incrementally. It's happening in overlapping waves: hyper-efficient AI tools that are reshaping what legal work can cost, firms making painful restructuring decisions in response, and corporations scrambling to figure out their strategy before their competitors move first.

At the center of this is Harvey, the AI legal assistant founded by former Google DeepMind and OpenAI engineers. Harvey has raised over $1 billion in funding and achieved a valuation exceeding $11 billion. Its enterprise clients include major law firms and Fortune 500 companies who are using it for contract review, legal research, and document drafting. The speed and accuracy improvements are not marginal — Harvey can review a 200-page contract in minutes that would take a team of paralegals days, and its legal research capabilities rival those of experienced associates.

But Harvey is far from alone in this space. The competitive landscape is stratified:

## The Tool Landscape

**Enterprise AI Powerhouses:**

- **Harvey** (valued at $11B) — The leader, backed by Andreessen Horowitz and Sequoia. Integrates deeply with law firm workflows, handles litigation support, M&A due diligence, and contract review
- **Casetext by Thomson Reuters** (Co-Counsel) — Leverages Westlaw's massive legal database. Strong for case law research and regulatory analysis
- **Ironclad** — AI-powered contract lifecycle management. Focuses on the entire contract workflow from drafting to execution to compliance
- **Luminance** — AI for contract analytics and due diligence. Already used by major firms for M&A transaction review

**Mid-Market & Specialized:**

- **Henchman** (now part of Thomson Reuters) — AI research assistant with deep integration into Casetext's platform
- **Eve** — AI legal assistant focused on workplace law and compliance
- **Kira Systems** — Contract intelligence and data extraction. Acquired by DAX Corporation

**For In-House Teams Specifically:**

- **Clio** — Practice management with AI features for small firms and solo practitioners
- **Relativity** — eDiscovery and litigation support with AI-powered document review
- **Docusign** — Contract execution with AI-powered data extraction and workflow automation
- **Ironclad** — Already mentioned above, but particularly relevant for in-house legal departments managing contract lifecycles at scale

## The Human Impact: What's Being Eliminated

The most controversial developments have come from the restructuring of large law firms. **Baker McKenzie**, one of the world's largest law firms, announced layoffs of approximately 700 support staff positions in 2025, explicitly citing AI automation as a contributing factor. This wasn't framed as "AI replacing lawyers" — it was positioned as eliminating support roles that AI tools now handle: document review, discovery, and routine legal work.

The pattern is becoming clear across the profession:

1. **Contract review and discovery** — AI can review thousands of documents faster and more accurately than junior associates and paralegals. This is the work that has been most rapidly automated
2. **Legal research** — What used to take associates hours of Westlaw or Westlaw-style research can now be completed by AI systems that synthesize relevant case law, statutes, and regulations
3. **Document drafting** — Template-driven contracts, NDAs, employment agreements — these are increasingly generated without human involvement

But there's a critical distinction emerging in the research. A **2024 Harvard Law School study** found that firms treating AI as a **workflow enhancement tool** (not a replacement for lawyers) reported measurable productivity gains — the question wasn't *if* AI should be adopted, but *how* to optimize its integration with human expertise.

## The Contrarian Trend: Legal Demand Is Still Surging

Here's the counter-intuitive data point that complicates the story: **workplace legal claims have surged over 70% in the past three years**, according to the Association of Corporate Counsels and regulatory filing data. Regulatory complexity is growing (not shrinking). The volume of legal work in areas like data privacy, employment law, and securities regulation is expanding.

What's changing is not the total demand for legal services — it's the **staffing model** required to serve that demand.

## What Companies Should Do: A Practical Playbook

For legal departments deciding how to position themselves for the AI transition, here are the five most critical considerations:

### 1. Deploy AI for High-Volume Work, Keep Humans on Judgment

The data is clear: AI excels at pattern recognition in contracts, document review, and research. Humans excel at judgment calls, negotiation strategy, and complex regulatory interpretation. The most successful departments are using AI to **absorb the volume work** (contract review, discovery, routine drafting) and reallocating that time toward higher-value legal strategy. This isn't just about cost savings — it's about what the department *can do* rather than what it *has to survive*.

### 2. Govern AI-Generated Output

You cannot deploy AI for legal work without a governance framework. AI-generated contracts should be reviewed by qualified attorneys before use. AI-generated research should be verified. The legal industry has always required accuracy — in law, inaccuracy creates liability. Treat AI output as **research assistance, not legal authority**.

### 3. Invest in AI-Adjacent Legal Skills

Department members who can effectively prompt, evaluate, and integrate AI tools are already more valuable than peers who haven't. This creates an investment imperative: **train your legal team on AI tool selection, evaluation, and strategic use**. Companies that invest in AI literacy within their legal departments will gain a competitive advantage that compounds over time.

### 4. Prepare for Organizational Impact

The restructuring at Baker McKenzie and similar firms signals what's coming across the industry. The work that paralegals and junior associates used to absorb is now automated. Legal departments should:
- Audit which functions are most vulnerable to automation
- Retrain staff rather than eliminate them (where possible)
- Build workflows that use AI to augment rather than replace existing talent

The **70% increase in workplace legal claims** means the department will still need headcount — but the *type* of headcount will shift toward roles that require judgment, negotiation, and complex legal reasoning.

### 5. Build Internal AI Literacy

The legal professionals who understand how AI works, its limitations, and where it adds value compared to traditional methods will define the next generation of legal department leadership. This isn't optional — it's the core competency that will determine which departments thrive and which are left managing older workflows while their competitors move faster.

## The Bottom Line

The legal AI transformation is not about whether AI will impact legal work — that's already happening. The question for legal departments is whether they'll approach AI as a strategic opportunity to **eliminate low-value work and invest in higher-value legal strategy** or as a threat to be managed defensively.

The departments that win will be the ones that deploy AI tools aggressively while simultaneously investing in their team's ability to use those tools effectively and govern the output appropriately. The competitive advantage won't come from having AI — it will come from knowing how to use it, governing it properly, and reallocating the freed time toward the legal judgment that only humans can provide.
