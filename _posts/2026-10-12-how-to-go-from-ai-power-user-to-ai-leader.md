# Summary: How to Go from AI Power User to AI Leader

[The AI Daily Brief - How to Go from AI Power User to AI Leader ](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Go-from-AI-Power-User-to-AI-Leader-e3qcev2)

In this Operators Cut episode, recorded the week before the US federal holiday of October 12, host Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW) and returning guest Nufar Gaspar -- an executive AI consultant who has run AI champion programs across dozens of enterprises -- make a bold claim: a well-structured internal AI champions program is the single highest-ROI transformation activity they have seen in enterprise AI, and it never fails. The episode is a full playbook for that claim, covering how to define the champion role, nominate and train for it, and why most champion networks die the day after certification. Gaspar is also launching an AI Champions bootcamp to train the role directly.

* **The problem champions exist to solve**: every team has someone who finds a great way to do something with AI, shows two people, and it never spreads to the whole team -- the champion is the person whose job is to fix that and spread the AI.
* **Definition -- "peer plus one"**: a champion sits within the team and does the team's work, but is a step ahead in AI; they show what AI can do on the work their peers actually do (real use cases, not theory), connect with other champions and central AI teams, and move people forward without having authority over them.
* **The role runs a spectrum**: at one end, ambassadors drive adoption, showcase real use cases, and voice what the team needs from tools and internal builds; at the other end, the internal FDE (forward-deployed engineer) is a professional builder embedded in a business team who takes solutions from idea all the way to production -- some with software backgrounds, many subject matter experts who made a genuine career pivot into building. Most companies land in the middle on ambassador-builders, who build within a defined scope and act like product managers beyond it.
* **Past intermediate, the role finds you**: NLW argues that once you're an advanced AI user you'll be thrust into the champion role in some form -- for friends and family at minimum -- and it's a different muscle than using AI well: communicating how to use it and thinking in process terms rather than just implementation.
* **Companies really ask four questions**: who gets to build (the democratization question), what is expected of regular users versus champions, how AI spreads from the field, and how champions work with central teams, vendors, IT, and security -- even if nobody in your company holds the title "central team," you are living with these questions.
* **Stage one -- define the mission**: ask what the network is meant to accomplish (companies struggle to answer), then decide where champions sit on the ambassador-to-FDE line; write down goals, responsibilities, and expectations, agreed with IT and security. One company realized it wanted champions primarily as a funnel bringing good use cases from the ground up to a central team otherwise blind to day-to-day reality -- a completely different role and training than breeding full builders.
* **Stage two -- nomination signals**: a good champion uses AI without being told to, their peers already come to them for guidance, and their manager actually gives them time to work on AI for others -- that last one is where great nominations most often fall short. Other traits: influence without authority, deep business knowledge, initiative, and curiosity.
* **Four nomination mistakes to never make**: senior people self-nominating to improve their skills or steer what gets built (they belong on an AI steering committee, not chartered to train peers), great candidates who are the busiest people on the team with no bandwidth, nomination as a reward for strong players who aren't actually good AI users, and nominating someone who just started yesterday -- champions must know the business ins and outs to spot use cases.
* **The training test**: whatever the format, train champions on their own work -- every champion takes one real use case from their team and shows how they built or promoted it by the end of training; if they can, they're ready. Certification kickoffs generate pride and excitement, but that's where many initiatives fail.
* **The day after is the biggest predictor of success**: in every company where an amazing champion course never materialized into business value, the missing piece was that nobody owned the continuation -- champions drift back to working the way they did before.
* **Every network needs two sponsors**: a community-side sponsor from HR/the people team who keeps the human side alive, and a professional sponsor savvy about AI who works closely with the chief AI officer, sets priorities, and clears the path on approvals, access, and tools. The most successful network Gaspar has seen runs on a dynamic duo of an L&D expert and an AI expert.
* **The network must be a living entity**: not everyone nominated will stay -- people leave, get busy, or realize the role isn't for them -- so make exits and onboarding easy, thank people who step out, and nominate new people constantly. If a network is losing energy: name your two sponsors, verify every champion still has one or two days of protected capacity, and reinstigate the commitment with their managers.
* **Champions must see the whole road**: use case travels from idea to production in three stretches -- before the build (knowing the ground, what the company allows, where use cases live), the build itself (POC to production), and after the build (value realization, deployment, retiring solutions when needed). Most people only know the middle.
* **The most important champion skill is evaluation**: how do you know what you built is good, when you can trust it, and what the mistakes cost -- the champion's defining judgment call is signing off that a thing is good enough to put in users' hands.
* **Playbook one -- should you even build it?**: huge bias toward building exists among individuals and companies; check frequency, value, and error tolerance, then decide between reuse, buy, build, or backlog -- decisions made from a grounded, company-connected point of view, not as a passion project.
* **Playbook two -- build the simplest thing that solves the problem**: sometimes a shared chat setup with prompt instructions and files is enough, sometimes a skill is the best way to make playbooks that run on demand, sometimes an automation, and only sometimes a full-blown agent; complexity you don't need is hard to build, maintain, and test.
* **Playbook three -- define core properties before building**: who is it for ("everyone" is not a good answer), what it needs to know (often surfacing an entire knowledge-curation project), how people will use it so it isn't a glorious vibe-coded app nobody remembers the URL of, concrete guardrails on what it must never do, where human judgment sits in the loop, and the test cases it must pass -- configured early, which often kills the wrong project before it's built.
* **Agentic coding makes champions more mission-critical, not less**: NLW sees the non-technical person using code and agentic coding tools as the sharpest current example of AI's power and challenge at once -- agents are powerful and tenacious enough to escape containment and wind their way into many systems. Gaspar's answer: don't shut down the less-professional builders; invest in enablement and infrastructure and train more subject matter experts, because they work on the right use cases -- and getting that right is harder than mastering the technology itself.
* **Sponsor segment -- KPMG/UT Austin "AI amplifiers" study**: a field study of 523 US-based early-career professionals (published in Harvard Business Review, July 2026, with UT Austin's McCombs School of Business) found nearly identical employees produce dramatically different results with AI: 50.1% "AI Amplifiers" outperformed the AI-only baseline, 25.8% "AI Delegators" matched it, and 24.1% "AI Apprentices" fell below it -- what separated them was how they worked with AI (orchestrating, evaluating, refining), not what they knew.

The tools will change every quarter, but the champion playbook doesn't: right people with protected time, a written decision on where they sit between ambassador and builder, training on their own real work, champions who understand the whole road from idea to production, and someone who owns the day after certification. For the individual power user, the takeaway is that stepping into the champion seat -- spotting use cases, articulating ROI, choosing the simplest solution, and being the person who can sign off on quality -- is one of the best positions in a company, and the role only grows more mission-critical as agentic coding spreads through organizations.
