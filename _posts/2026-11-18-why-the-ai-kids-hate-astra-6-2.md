# Summary: Why the AI Kids Hate Astra 6.2

[The AI Daily Brief - Why the AI Kids Hate Astra 6.2](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/Why-the-AI-Kids-Hate-Astra-6-2-e3sl38t)

OpenAI shipped GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol on Tuesday, and the early-tester reaction was some of the worst any model release has ever drawn. Host Nathaniel Whittemore digs into whether the models are actually bad or whether OpenAI is deliberately building for something the early-adopter crowd doesn't care about -- efficiency, cost, and everyday professional work rather than coding. The episode also covers the wave of outages reaching the NSA's classified network and Jensen Huang's public bid to return as Nvidia CEO, which the board is publicly rejecting even as a faction inside it reportedly wants him back.

* **The release:** GPT-6.2 Astra (frontier model for hard projects) and GPT-6.2 Sol (efficient everyday model) launched Tuesday, pitched as "super efficient and built to perform where it counts, the real world" -- leading with Agents' Last Exam and Terminal-Bench-Science rather than the coding benchmarks that topped every release for the past year.
* **The backlash was brutal:** Matt Shumer said he wouldn't even write a full review because the models were "essentially unusable"; developer Theo Browne filled entire sections with complaints that the model "speaks in tongues that only 6.2 Astra understands" and runs in loops; Wharton's Ethan Mollick called it "very uneven and weird," the first OpenAI frontier release since GPT-4.5 that wasn't a clear step forward.
* **Every's 30-person team: zero switched:** Dan Shipper's team tested the models for weeks on real coding, writing, design, and knowledge work -- no one who tried Astra 6.2 switched back to it. Writing was trained so concise it became hard to follow, coding stopped too early on end-to-end builds, and designs fell into a noticeable house style.
* **The benchmarks actually improved:** On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, Sol rose from 55 to 57 (tying GPT-6 Astra, still behind Opus 5.5, Gemini 4 Carbon, and Fable 5.2) and Astra gained five points to 62, second only to Fable 5.2. AA says OpenAI now holds solid control of most of the Pareto frontier at mid-range prices, with Anthropic a bit smarter at the premium end and Meta winning on the cheapest models.
* **The cost story is the real pitch:** Astra 6.2 completed AA's benchmark run at $4 per task versus $6.24 for GPT-6 Astra -- a 35% cut from token efficiency plus per-token price cuts. Sol fell to $1.01 from $1.27. Fable 5.2 on high settings still costs more than twice what Astra costs on max, and 6.2 Sol on max is less than a third the cost of Opus 5.5 on high.
* **The red flag that set the narrative:** both models regressed on AA's coding agent index -- Astra held its score while Sol dropped three points, slipping behind Kimi 3.1 and GPT-6.1 Terra, driven by a SWE Atlas regression where the model omits key facts. The question is whether testers found the model bad, or just worse at the one thing they cared about.
* **The other camp loved it:** Ash praised it for computer use and agentic video editing; Claire Vo built an interactive storybook and called Astra 6.2 the winner over Opus and Fable on image generation and animation code; Josh Purtell called both models "absolute monsters" on his auto-research benchmark; Alex Finn finished his benchmark in a third of the time at a third of the price using a quarter of the tokens; swyx said it's a win on price-to-output and that he daily-drives Astra for Latent Space's publishing pipeline.
* **The harness question:** OpenAI's Thibault Sottiaux said the team gave early testers access weeks ago, then made "a lot of changes in the last three to four days" with no retesting -- critics called it self-sabotage. Theo retested afterward and landed on "better than when I tested, but still directionally very bad."
* **An old interaction-style split, now sharper:** Anthropic's Fable-class models are the kind you hand an entire assignment and walk away from; OpenAI models remain tuned for close back-and-forth collaboration. 6.2 may extend that pattern -- and look worse by comparison because Opus 5.5 is excellent at both styles.
* **NLW's verdict:** not a slam dunk, not era-defining -- a very incremental release with specific tuning decisions that either don't work in general or made the model less useful for certain use cases. Test it yourself; he's switching his own OpenAI workflows over to find out.
* **Not an IPO sabotage attempt:** OpenAI couldn't have thought this release would steal Anthropic's thunder after Fable 5.2. The likelier motive was defensive -- having a recent release during IPO chatter beats looking scared after a long silence -- with a third possibility: deliberately drawing a contrast with Anthropic for the post-IPO enterprise market.
* **The launch ad backfired:** a two-minute Office Space-style film shows white-collar workers using Astra to finish forgotten boss requests in under a minute. Mo called it "a mockery of humanity," Brian Lovin called it "a profoundly sad vision of the future," Zach Trader summed it up as "we help your incompetent people fake it," and swyx's primary critique was that nobody with decision-making power vetoed the ad.
* **The outage wave reaches the NSA:** after Microsoft Outlook, the FAA, and major US banks went down within a week, the NSA's classified internal network (NSANet) went down Tuesday, the campus reverted to essential personnel only, and Apple and United Airlines also had outages the same day. The NSA blamed "scheduled routine maintenance," and sources again said there's no evidence of a cyberattack.
* **The less scary AI theory:** ONCD director Sean Cairncross told reporter Ashley Gold the disruptions come from updating and modernizing legacy systems -- "the industry's known this for a while, it's just that it's manifesting." a16z's Martin Casado drew a Y2K parallel, and Mackenzie Arnold noted AI is making these upgrades affordable and feasible for the first time: since Mythos/Fable-level models arrived, companies have been racing to patch 30-year-old systems held together with popsicle sticks and bubblegum, all at once, with specialists stretched thin.
* **Jensen says he'd come back:** in his first statement since resigning last Thursday, Huang posted that he made "serious mistakes in my personal life," owns them completely, and will cooperate with the board's independent investigation -- and that he'd be "honored" to return as CEO if the board decides it's in the best interest of shareholders, employees, the company, and America. Nvidia stock rose 2% in the five minutes after the post; Paul Graham said he envied no one more than an Nvidia board member claiming with a straight face that another candidate would do better.
* **The board says no -- reportedly not unanimously:** new board chair Harvey Jones opened the earnings call making clear the gears are not turning, and interim CEO Colette Kress had already told CNBC the board determined it will not reinstate Huang. But Semafor reported a faction of board members is pushing for his return -- Huang spoke with at least one director before his post; three members are said to be vehemently opposed and the other seven unclear.
* **Nvidia's first post-Jensen quarter was a blowout:** 103% annualized revenue growth, a raised Q4 projection of $130 billion, and growth forecasts out to 2028 lifted from 70% to 75%. Kress closed the call with "We've got this," and the stock rose 4.5% after hours to a post-Jensen high.
* **China door closes further:** Kress said guidance includes no licensed Hopper data center products and Nvidia is no longer shipping them to China even with government approval -- and the report doesn't yet reflect revenue lost from shutting down smuggling routes. Board chair Jones acknowledged "national security dynamics are making major changes in our marketplace," a world the next CEO will have to navigate carefully.

The Astra 6.2 backlash may say more about OpenAI's direction than about its models: the release regressed on exactly what early adopters care about most -- coding -- while cutting cost per task by up to 35% and pitching agents, efficiency, and everyday professional work. That may be a deliberate attempt to carve out a different place in the enterprise market while Anthropic owns the premium coding lane. Perception is in the eye of the beholder, so test it yourself.
