# Summary: The Best Way to Test New AI Models

[The AI Daily Brief - The Best Way to Test New AI Models](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/The-Best-Way-to-Test-New-AI-Models-e3q3meu)

NLW (Nathaniel Whittemore) and guest Nufar Gaspar record this Operator's Cut as a live webinar: a repeatable, five-step system for building your own personal AI benchmark so you can test new models against the tasks you actually do instead of trusting published scores. It lands in the middle of what they call the fall glut of new models, and they pair the framework with Nufar's own blind taste-test of seven frontier candidates across six real work tasks -- with surprising winners and a cost curve that upended her expectations.

* **The problem: benchmarks are decaying**: NLW's standard caution is not that any lab is lying, but that most published benchmarks are now inside training sets, so their value decays the longer they stick around -- and as of October a new frontier model lands on average roughly every 11 days, which no one can adopt blindly.
* **Fit matters more than rank**: Even if a benchmark were perfect, the real question is how a model feels and how it fits the harness around you, and NLW argues that the model just below state-of-the-art on the chart is sometimes the better one for a specific job, especially where output quality is subjective like writing.
* **No shortcut but using it**: His bottom line is that there is no substitute for running your own work through a new model -- which is exactly why Nufar built this shareable system and will hand over her script, the interview prompt, and her scoring handouts.
* **Step 1 -- pick your tasks (the most consequential choice)**: Choose four or six use cases that mirror your real workload, weighted toward high-frequency tasks where a good read actually matters and biased toward work versus personal only to the extent that matches how you use AI.
* **Include at least one wishlist task**: Deliberately add a use case you have tried with AI and been unhappy with, so a new model's real test is whether it unblocks something on your backburner rather than only confirming what already works.
* **Step 2 -- shortlist the candidates**: Test the new model against your current incumbent as the baseline, or up to four or five candidates at once when several drop in a wave -- Nufar warns against the seven she ran, describing it as "extremely tedious" to score.
* **Step 3 -- anonymize and score blind**: Hide which model produced which output (via a colleague, a spreadsheet randomizer, an AI shuffle, or her own script), run every candidate from a fresh chat window to avoid context bleed, and score side by side rather than in isolation because absolute 1-5 ratings are hard and relative rankings are not.
* **Step 4 -- let a second family judge it**: Add an AI judge from a different model family to score against a rubric (Nufar used Gemini Pro because none of her candidates were Gemini), while flagging the built-in "AI nepotism" of judges favoring same-family outputs -- and evaluating the judge itself, since a judge that does not correlate with your taste is just noise.
* **Step 5 -- decide deliberately**: The outcome is one of three -- Switch, Split (different model per task), or Stay -- and it is weighted by cost, latency, company policy, plan generosity, and the real cost of retraining your own workflow, not quality alone.
* **Three ways to run it**: Fully manual with a trusted person anonymizing the outputs; Nufar's Python script, which hits OpenRouter or direct vendor APIs, anonymizes results A through G, hosts a simple local scoring page, and auto-tracks per-candidate cost and latency; or a dedicated platform for teams evaluating at scale, naming LangSmith, Brain Trust, and Langfuse.
* **Nufar's six tasks**: a LinkedIn post, a pricing-pushback stakeholder email, tool-feature research with web search, a course participant hub website (the visual task), a short new course exercise, and a one-shot weekly plan (the wishlist item).
* **Her seven candidates + judge**: Claude Opus 5 as the baseline, then Claude Opus 5.5, Grok, GPT-6 Sol, Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra, and open-weights Kimi K3 -- scored blind by both Nufar and Gemini Pro.
* **LinkedIn -- no clean winner**: She gave none of the seven a five, and her top three were Kimi K3, Fable, and Opus, with the models she expected on top (GPT) missing the tier entirely.
* **Pricing pushback -- GPT leads**: The negotiation-writing task was the one where her ranking and Gemini's judge agreed, with the GPT family ahead -- confirming her prior that GPT is the strongest negotiator of the bunch.
* **Weekly planning (the wishlist) -- Grok won**: The intricate one-shot planning task that no model had reliably delivered for her before went to Grok, a result she called genuinely interesting.
* **Website design -- Opus 5.5 led visually**: On the course hub, where the rendered output mattered most, she picked Opus 5.5, and all seven produced surprisingly complete, navigable HTML -- which also made it the easiest to score because visual outputs are easier to rank than prose.
* **Cost and speed told a different story**: Fable 5.1 took the Gemini judge's top spot on aggregate but at a cost an order of magnitude above GPT-6 Sol (which matched Fable on Nufar's own personal score at a fraction of the price), Grok was the slowest by far despite a manageable cost, and Kimi K3 won on both cost and latency while scoring lower overall.
* **The "surprise" of the reveal**: Nufar -- a self-described heavy Claude user who is "biased towards Claude and a little bit with Grok" -- found GPT more competitive than expected and said she is actively weighing a shift, a conclusion she credits entirely to the blind test.
* **Human vs AI judge**: Nufar and Gemini Pro disagreed on four of the five scored tasks (pricing pushback was the exception); the sharpest gap was on the visual site, where Gemini could only judge the code rather than the rendered page -- an argument that text-only judging is incomplete for visual work.
* **The hidden cost, and the easy one**: She cautions that a generous subscription (Pro or Teams tier) can already put you "inside the cost" of the top model so the API price table overstates your personal delta -- but those tiers are not guaranteed to stay generous, and if you regularly hit rate limits the cost question is live and you should weight it heavily.
* **Keep the effort settings comparable**: Models have different "effort" or thinking-depth modes, so the correct apples-to-apples move is to align them across candidates, or at minimum be explicit that you are comparing everyone's default.
* **The KPMG / University of Texas angle (sponsor)**: A new study of 500+ early-career professionals found that the best outcomes with AI come from guiding, evaluating, and refining the output -- what KPMG calls "AI amplifiers" -- rather than from raw domain knowledge alone, which is the exact muscle a personal benchmark trains.
* **Speed is a moving target**: In the Q&A, Nufar says her own patience threshold shifted after OpenAI demoed its new ultra-fast mode (on the $500/month Ultra plan) and after she watched a single website render drag on for 20 minutes mid-test -- evidence that "how good looks" and "how fast is acceptable" both keep ratcheting up as the frontier moves.

The takeaway: published benchmarks are necessary but not sufficient, and the only defensible answer to "should I switch?" is a repeatable personal benchmark built around your own tasks. Nufar's five steps -- choose representative use cases, shortlist candidates, anonymize and score blind, cross-check with a second-family judge, then decide on cost, latency, and habit as well as quality -- is the concrete mechanism, and her own results (GPT more competitive than expected, Fable 5.1 highest on the aggregate but at a steep price premium, Grok the surprise planning winner) show that the "best model" is genuinely personal and often not the one with the top chart score.
