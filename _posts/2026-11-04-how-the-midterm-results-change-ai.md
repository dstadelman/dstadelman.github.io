# Summary: How the Midterm Results Change AI

[AI Daily Brief - How the Midterm Results Change AI](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-the-Midterm-Results-Change-AI-e3rotli)

The 2026 midterms delivered the promised blue wave -- Democrats took both the House and the Senate on an 11-point national swing -- and host Nathaniel Whittemore examines what it means for AI. The core finding: AI itself was not on the ballot, data centers were, and supporting them was a vote loser in both parties. His take is that the election did not change the direction of AI policy so much as confirm it, and that the real battleground has shifted to statehouses and the 2028 cycle.

* **A genuine blue wave, with limits**: Democrats posted an 11-point swing and will take the House and Senate, though unlikely with a veto-proof supermajority; the seat count trails the 2018 and 2010 wipeouts, but Democrats flipped unlikely seats in Texas, South Carolina, and Kentucky.
* **AI wasn't on the ballot -- data centers were**: Pew's early-October poll found only 44% of voters called AI a very important issue, versus 59% immigration, 68% health care, 73% the future of democracy, and 84% the economy; the issue skewed Democratic (54% vs. 34%).
* **Pro-data-center candidates were toxic on both sides**: 61% of voters said they disliked or strongly disliked candidates supporting local data centers -- 75% of Democratic supporters and 44% of Republican supporters -- while only 5% of Democrats and 14% of Republicans liked them.
* **Exit polls put data centers last among hot-button issues**: 56% called them a crucial or major voting factor, behind immigration enforcement (73%), the war in Iran (74%), cost of living (89%), and government corruption (90%).
* **Trump's "backwards and poor" line gave Republicans an opening few took**: after Trump said in late August that communities rejecting data centers would end up backwards and poor, most GOP candidates in tight races stuck with the status quo -- like Ohio's Jon Husted saying data centers should remain a local decision.
* **Ohio's Senate race ran almost entirely on data centers**: with 46 planned projects totaling 16 GW -- including Meta's gigawatt-scale Prometheus near Columbus and OpenAI's planned 10 GW Pike County campus -- Sherrod Brown labeled Husted "the face of data centers" and ran an ad that was nothing but the hum of a data center; Brown won, returning to the Senate with 18 years of veteran experience and a grudge after tech money helped unseat him in 2024.
* **SB 1047's author is headed to Congress from AI's home district**: in California's 11th -- Pelosi's old seat containing OpenAI and Anthropic headquarters -- Scott Wiener held a commanding lead over Pelosi-endorsed Connie Chan, having boasted he "went to war with Big Tech" and taken $3M from Anthropic-aligned PAC Public First Action; he's positioned as a champion for existential-risk-focused AI safety advocates.
* **Texas flipped, and the governor's race matters most for AI**: James Talarico beat Ken Paxton to become Texas's first Democratic senator since 1993, but the bigger AI story was Gina Hinojosa unseating three-term Governor Greg Abbott, ending 30 years of GOP control in a state that had 470+ GW of data center proposals -- roughly three times its grid -- when Abbott's September permitting freeze came too late.
* **The statehouses may matter more than Washington**: with Ohio and Georgia also flipping, nine of the ten largest states are now run by Democrats (Florida is the lone top-10 GOP state), and data center policy is set by governors; Hinojosa's platform includes a moratorium until rules are written, ending tax subsidies she called a "corruption tax," and protecting local electricity and water.
* **Her key line frames what comes next**: at an August press conference Hinojosa said, "I don't hate data centers. I just hate the way they're taking advantage of Texans" -- the gap between those two sentiments is the practical space where developers and new leaders can strike a deal.
* **Ossoff and El-Sayed are the rising anti-data-center stars**: Jon Ossoff cruised to a second term over Mike Collins in Georgia, tying data centers to oligarchy and claiming Trump's big beautiful bill gave $70B to data center developers, fueling 2028 chatter; Michigan's Abdul El-Sayed -- the first Muslim senator in US history -- told 60 Minutes that AI is now the issue he hears about most, from job loss to bioweapons, and is a policy wild card.
* **All 41 local data center ballot measures look set to pass**: Whittemore reads the clean sweep as proof the industry has "completely lost the narrative war," with Americans for Responsible Innovation co-founder Brad Carson calling the results "an AI earthquake."
* **Comprehensive federal regulation remains unlikely**: Trump told Fox News that on a Democratic win "I guess I'll become Mr. Veto," so no top-down federal moratorium on data centers or frontier training is expected despite the Democratic sweep.
* **The likely wins are common-sense, bipartisan rules**: whistleblower protections for lab employees, codifying voluntary pre-release testing of frontier models, incident reporting requirements, stronger liability for model builders, and possibly data sovereignty rules.
* **Export controls are the wild card, and RSI enters the agenda**: China policy doesn't split cleanly by party, so no easy extrapolation from the Democratic win; expect debate over pausing, pacing, or prohibiting techniques like recursive self-improvement, plus many executive hearings that mostly won't matter.
* **The shift toward more rules was inevitable anyway**: beyond the rejection of Trump, more powerful models naturally ramp the controls people want -- the correlation between capability and regulation "just makes sense" to many voters regardless of who won.
* **The era of ignoring local communities is already over**: developers spent all year dropping NDAs, committing to pay their own way on infrastructure and electricity, and adding community sweeteners -- and from first principles of fairness, that arrangement is closer to the right one than the deal of a couple years ago.
* **The disaster scenario**: moratoria accelerate, new governors act fast, Wall Street pulls funding, neoclouds go bust, and a broader downturn follows because the AI buildout has been "pretty much all we've had going for us" -- setting up a 2028 election fought over a burst bubble.
* **Bottom line -- did anything change? Sort of**: the night mostly confirmed the direction things were already heading, and Whittemore sees tons of political room for alignment, calling the data center buildout "the most winnable political issue I've ever seen" -- winnable for everyone -- unless the 2028 crop decides that being virulently anti-AI pays, in which case "I could be eating those words."

The blue wave didn't change AI's political direction; it confirmed it. Data centers -- not AI in the abstract -- were the issue voters punished, and with Democrats now governing nine of the ten largest states and every local data center measure passing, the industry faces a new era where community accommodation is mandatory. The fight ahead is incremental federal rules, state-level permitting fights, and a 2028 cycle that starts now.
