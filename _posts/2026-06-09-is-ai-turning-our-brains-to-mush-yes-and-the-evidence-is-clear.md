title: "Is AI Turning Our Brains to Mush? Yes -- and the Evidence Is Clear"

A 2025 MIT Media Lab study put 54 college students in a four-month experiment tracking how their brains responded to AI-assisted writing. [1] One group used ChatGPT. One used Google search. One worked without any tools. ChatGPT users showed the weakest neural connectivity across all measured brain regions, up to 55% lower than the unaided writers. The effect did not just disappear when they stopped using the tool. Months later, when reassigned to write without AI, the former AI users exhibited a persistent suppression of alpha and theta brain waves. The researchers called it "cognitive debt" because the users were carrying a neurological deficit they had not built up by exercising those circuits. [1]

Neural pathways that fire repeatedly strengthen. Those that sit idle atrophy. The MIT team's point was straightforward: when you outsource structuring an argument, retrieving vocabulary, or building a line of reasoning to an LLM, those circuits do not get used. The deficit compounds because you are not skipping a single instance of mental work. You are bypassing the deep memory integration that turns information into knowledge [1].

The number from that study that matters most is 83%. When asked to recall a passage they had just written minutes earlier with ChatGPT, 83% of AI users could not accurately describe or quote what they had produced [5]. The task was completed. The essay existed on screen. The writer had no integrated memory of it. They had not learned anything by producing it.

Other labs are finding convergent patterns. A 2025 study in Frontiers in Psychology tracked 64 participants doing creative design work with AI tools and found significant reductions in cognitive engagement across prefrontal and parietal regions associated with creativity and problem solving [7]. A separate bioRxiv preprint used fMRI on children ages 6 to 7 alongside adults doing the same AI interaction tasks and found that children's cognitive control and attention networks were even more suppressed than adults' [6]. The developing brain appears especially vulnerable. This is why MIT lead author Nataliya Kosmyna pushed the findings out before peer review: she was watching policymakers consider putting ChatGPT in kindergartens and wanted to sound the alarm while there was still time [4].

The mechanism is well-studied outside of AI. Cognitive offloading -- the practice of reducing mental effort by outsourcing to an external tool -- was documented in the famous 2011 Google Effect study, which showed that people remember less when they know information will be available online [8]. The researchers then demonstrated a principle from neuroscience: when memory is not exercised, the associated neural networks weaken. Generative AI turns offloading from episodic into chronic.

There is also the Extended Mind Theory to consider. Philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers argued that external tools can become cognitive prosthetics, part of the thinking system itself [7]. When the tool becomes unreliable -- and chatbots hallucinate -- the prosthetic stops functioning properly and the underlying cognitive muscles, weakened from disuse, cannot compensate. The person does not just produce wrong output. They lack the capacity to detect that it is wrong. A 2025 Microsoft/NBER survey of knowledge workers found that users' self-reported cognitive effort declined sharply with increased AI use, and that this correlated with lower confidence in their own judgments [9]. They were getting faster, but they were also getting less certain they knew what they were doing.

Harvard Kennedy School's Dan Levy puts it directly: no learning occurs unless the brain is actively engaged in making meaning, and AI can easily be used to short-circuit that engagement [10]. You ask it for the answer and your brain receives the result without the struggle that makes it stick. His co-author Angela Perez Albertos adds that there is simply no such thing as "AI is good for learning" or "AI is bad for learning" as a blanket statement -- the outcome depends entirely on whether the user does the work or lets the machine do it for them. The question is whether people generally have the discipline to do the work when a frictionless alternative is one click away [10].

Occasional, deliberate use of AI is not necessarily harmful. The MIT study found that users who started without AI and then later adopted ChatGPT actually showed increased brain connectivity during that transition. They had strong cognitive foundations to build on [1]. The damage path runs in the other direction. Start with AI and decline is structural.

The HAL research paper on "Generational Cognitive Atrophy in the Age of AI" warns that prolonged dependence on AI systems is associated with diminished working memory, weakened metacognitive regulation, and eroded analytical habits across whole cohorts of younger users. [6] Working memory is the limited workspace where you hold and manipulate information before committing it to long-term memory. When working memory shrinks across a generation, you do not get a generation of people who are slightly less sharp. You get a generation whose entire capacity for deep, sustained reasoning is compressed.

Neural pathways atrophy when they are systematically bypassed and never activated. The evidence from multiple independent labs all points in the same direction, and the mechanism is well understood in neuroscience. The question is how fast it happens and how many of us are willing to keep using tools that make us think less while pretending we are thinking more.
