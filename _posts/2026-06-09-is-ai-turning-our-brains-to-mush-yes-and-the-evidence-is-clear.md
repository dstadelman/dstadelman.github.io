Is AI Turning Our Brains to Mush? Yes -- and the Evidence Is Clear

The most damning number from a 2025 MIT Media Lab study is 55 percent. That is how much weaker neural connectivity became in students writing essays with ChatGPT compared to students writing without any tools. What followed was worse: months later, when researchers reassigned the ChatGPT users to write without AI, those students exhibited persistent suppression of alpha and theta brain waves. The MIT researchers called it cognitive debt, a neurological deficit the students had accumulated simply by using the tool [1].

Neural pathways that fire repeatedly strengthen. Those that sit idle atrophy. The math does not bend here: when you outsource structuring an argument, retrieving vocabulary, or building a line of reasoning to an LLM, those circuits do not get used. You are not skipping a single instance of mental work. You are bypassing the deep memory integration that turns information into knowledge [1].

The 83 percent from that same study is what it actually means in practice. When asked to recall a passage they had written minutes earlier with ChatGPT, 83 percent of those AI users could not accurately describe or quote what they had produced. The essay existed on their screen. They had no integrated memory of it. They had learned nothing by producing it [5].

Other labs are finding the same pattern. Frontiers in Psychology tracked 64 participants doing creative design work with AI tools and found significant reductions in cognitive engagement across prefrontal and parietal regions linked to creativity and problem solving [7]. A bioRxiv preprint used fMRI on children ages 6 to 7 alongside adults doing the same AI tasks and found that children's cognitive control and attention networks were even more suppressed than adults'. The developing brain appears especially vulnerable [6]. This is why MIT lead author Nataliya Kosmyna pushed the findings out before peer review: she was watching policymakers consider putting ChatGPT in kindergartens and wanted to warn them while there was still time [4].

The mechanism was already documented decades before ChatGPT existed. Cognitive offloading -- reducing mental effort by outsourcing to an external tool -- was captured in the 2011 Google Effect study, which showed people remember less when they know information will be available online [8]. When memory is not exercised, the associated neural networks weaken. Generative AI turns episodic offloading into chronic use.

Extended Mind Theory adds another layer. Philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers argued that external tools become cognitive prosthetics, part of the thinking system itself [7]. A prosthetic that breaks down is merely inconvenient. A cognitive prosthetic that works intermittently -- and chatbots hallucinate regularly -- is dangerous. The underlying cognitive muscles, weakened from disuse, cannot compensate. The person does not just produce wrong output. They lack the capacity to detect that it is wrong [10].

A 2025 Microsoft/NBER study of knowledge workers confirmed this with survey data. Users' self-reported cognitive effort declined sharply with increased AI use, and that decline correlated with lower confidence in their own judgments. They were getting faster, but they were also getting less certain they knew what they were doing [9].

Dan Levy of the Harvard Kennedy School puts it bluntly: no learning occurs unless the brain is actively engaged in making meaning, and AI short-circuits that engagement. His co-author Angela Perez Albertos adds that the outcome depends entirely on whether the user does the work or lets the machine do it for them. The harder question is whether people have the discipline to do the work when frictionless alternatives live one click away [10].

There is one nuance that matters. The MIT study found that users who began writing without AI and only later adopted ChatGPT actually showed increased brain connectivity during that transition. They had strong cognitive foundations already in place to build on. Start with AI and decline is structural [1].

HAL's research paper on generational cognitive atrophy warns that prolonged AI dependence is associated with diminished working memory, weakened metacognitive regulation, and eroded analytical habits across cohorts of younger users. Working memory is the limited workspace where you hold and manipulate information before committing it to long-term memory. When working memory shrinks across an entire generation, the result is not a few people who are slightly duller. It is a generation whose capacity for deep, sustained reasoning is compressed [6].

Neural pathways atrophy when they are systematically bypassed. The evidence from independent labs -- MIT, Polytechnique, Frontiers, HAL, Microsoft/NBER, Harvard Kennedy School -- all points in the same direction. The mechanism is well understood in neuroscience. Every time someone uses AI to bypass the struggle that makes knowledge stick, that pathway weakens a little more.

The question is not whether AI will be used. It is what happens when the majority of people never learn to write without it.
