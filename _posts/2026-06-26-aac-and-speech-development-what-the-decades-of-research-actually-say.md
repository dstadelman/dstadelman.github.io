For decades, one myth has haunted parents of non-speaking children: *if you give them a device to communicate, they'll never try to talk.* It's the question every parent gets asked. Every therapist hears it too. Even well-meaning relatives say it unapologetically.

But that fear stands in direct opposition to what decades of rigorous research has shown. This is a deep-dive look at what the evidence actually says, what major professional organizations recommend, and why the myth persists despite having zero scientific support.

---

## The Short Answer Up Front

Let's state it clearly: **No peer-reviewed systematic review, meta-analysis, or position statement from any major professional organization has found that AAC delays speech development.**

In fact, the opposite is true. The preponderance of evidence shows either increased speech production after introducing AAC or no change in speech outcomes. Zero credible study has shown a decline.

---

## The Evidence FOR: What the Research Shows AAC Does

### 1. The Landmark Meta-Analysis by Millar, Light, and Schlosser (2006)

This is the gold-standard reference on this question. Published in *Augmentative and Alternative Communication* and indexed by NCBI as part of their critical review database, the researchers asked a very tight question: **What happens to oral speech production after AAC intervention?**

They found results from 27 individual cases and calculated Percentile Non-overlapping Data (PND) scores across them:

- **89% showed increase in** speech production
- **11% showed no change** in speech
- **0% showed decrease in speech**

Of the cases that did improve, 19 of 24 instances saw gains within just 5 sessions of the intervention. Where word counts were measured, the mean gain was 13 words (range: 1–52).

This study examined data from studies published between 1975 and 2003, covering 67 participants with a range of developmental disabilities including Down syndrome, autism, and intellectual disability. Both aided AAC (devices with symbols) and unaided AAC (signing, gestures) were included.

**Citation:** Millar DC, Light JC, Schlosser R. The impact of augmentative and alternative communication intervention on the speech production of individuals with developmental disabilities: a research review. *Augmentative and Alternative Communication*. 2006;22(2). Available from: NCBI Bookshelf, DBAEE Reviews (NBK72988).

### 2. Schlosser and Wendt's Systematic Review Update (2008)

Schlosser and Wendt published a systematic review specifically on this question in the *American Journal of Speech-Language Pathology*, updating Millar/Light/Schlosser's work with newer studies from 2004–2007. Their conclusion was consistent: AAC was either neutral or beneficial to speech outcomes, never detrimental.

**Citation:** Schlosser RW, Wendt OM. Effects of augmentative and alternative communication intervention on speech production: A systematic review. *American Journal of Speech-Language Pathology*. 2008;17(3):212-230.

### 3. White et al.'s ASD-Focused Systematic Review (2021)

This study directly replicated, updated, and extended Schlosser & Wendt's work for individuals specifically on the autism spectrum. Published in the *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*, it analyzed 25 single-case design articles and 3 group-design articles published between 1975 and May 2020.

Their findings: **"Overall, AAC resulted in improved speech production; however, speech gains that did occur did not surpass AAC use."** In other words, people communicated MORE with the system they used. They may have spoken less because they now had a tool that worked—but importantly, they didn't lose their ability to communicate overall. Their frustration levels dropped. Their functional communication increased.

The critical reframing here: *less* speech in favor of an AAC device isn't a loss—it's a substitution with something effective.

**Citation:** White E, Ayres K, Snyder S, Cagliani R, Ledford J. Augmentative and Alternative Communication and Speech Production for Individuals with ASD: A Systematic Review. *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*. 2021;51(12):4199-4212.

### 4. Recent Systematic Reviews on AAC for Ages 0–6 (2022)

Leonet et al. published a systematic review in *Language, Speech, and Hearing Services in Schools* evaluating AAC interventions for children ages 0 to 6 with various disabilities. Of 1,709 studies screened, 29 met inclusion criteria. The findings showed improvements across:

- Expressive communication skills
- Receptive communication skills  
- Functional communication behaviors
- Communication participation
- Interaction strategies
- Symbol production and comprehension

Again, no decline in any outcome domain was reported.

**Citation:** Leonet O, Orcasitas-Vicandi M, Langarika-Rocafort A, Idoiaga Mondragon N, Roman Etxebarrieta G. A Systematic Review of Augmentative and Alternative Communication Interventions for Children Aged From 0 to 6 Years. *Language, Speech, and Hearing Services in Schools*. 2022;53(3):894-920.

### 5. ASHA Evidence Maps on AAC for Childhood Apraxia of Speech (CAS)

ASHA's own evidence map research on children with childhood apraxia of speech found that **all studies demonstrated positive speech production outcomes and improved functional language skills** when using AAC interventions, including early intervention approaches like EACA (Enhancing Communication with Energy Above the Ear).

Research shows children with complex communication needs do best when introduced to AAC *as early as 12 months*—not after they've "outgrown" a wait-and-see period for speech.

## The Evidence AGAINST: Where Does the Concern Come From?

### What Researchers and Clinicians ACTUALLY Observed

The concern likely stems from two observable phenomema being misread as causation:

1. **Reduced attempts at unintelligible speech:** When a child can now communicate effectively through an AAC device, they naturally stop struggling to produce sounds that no one understands. Parents and observers interpret this drop as "loss" or "giving up on speech." It is not—it's the rational behavior of someone who found a system that works. A person who can use email doesn't start shouting across rooms to get attention anymore.

2. **Confusion between correlation and causation:** Children who are non-speaking tend to be offered AAC devices *because* they don't have enough speech yet. Therefore it looks like "AAC came in, then speech stopped." The temporal sequence is misinterpreted as causality when the relationship is actually: severe speech impairment → AAC intervention (not speech-impaired-because-of-AAC).

### Where Did the Myth Come From?

The myth has three contributing factors:

- **Historical lack of evidence.** Before the 1980s, there simply wasn't rigorous research on AAC. In the absence of data, assumption fills the gap—and the most natural assumption for a parent watching their child point at an AAC device instead of vocalizing is alarming.
- **Provider bias.** The AAP's 2025 clinical report explicitly identified "the ableism of some providers" as a barrier to children receiving AAC. Many healthcare professionals personally fear that prescribing AAC means admitting they'll never talk. This creates resistance at the prescription stage, depriving children of the tools they need during their most critical learning window.
- **Public visibility.** Speech-language pathologists have long stated this concern is pervasive enough that it requires active myth-busting. ASHA's own practice portal explicitly lists "potential AAC users may believe that AAC reduces motivation to improve natural speech and delays language development" as a misconception they actively work to counter.

## What the Major Organizations Say

### The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) — Clinical Report, June 2025

In their clinical report titled *"Prescribing Assistive Technology: Focus on Children With Complex Communication Needs,"* published in *Pediatrics*:

- The AAP **encourages pediatricians to actively prescribe AAC devices** for children who are non-verbal or have complex communication needs
- AAP explicitly states they can help parents understand "the role AAC plays in helping non-verbal children to communicate more effectively"
- The report calls on pediatricians to advocate for Medicaid coverage and counters the misconception that all AAC products are prohibitively expensive

AAP specifically identifies these conditions as benefiting: cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder, congenital anomalies, deafness, intellectual disabilities, brain injuries, severe speech disorders (including dyspraxia and childhood apraxia of speech), and genetic syndromes (Down syndrome, Rett syndrome).

The AAP report is notable for its insistence that pediatricians should not abdicate this duty. It states: "It can feel overwhelming to address these complex issues, but abdicating this duty to others is its own form of ableism we must combat."

**Citation:** American Academy of Pediatrics. Prescribing Assistive Technology: Focus on Children With Complex Communication Needs. *Pediatrics*. 2025;156(1):e2025072216. Also available at publications.aap.org and AAP pediatrician news coverage (AAP News #32384).

### The American Academy for the Advancement of Therapy for Communication Disabilities (AAACD) / NATE Network

The NATE Network explicitly states: **"No, the use of AAC including voice output devices absolutely does not prevent the development of speech. Some of the best evidence for this comes from a thorough meta-analysis of the literature by Millar, Light, and Schlosser (2006)."**

This is directly quoted from their official AAC resource page.

### The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA)

ASHA's practice portal on AAC explicitly identifies the delay myth as a misconception to counter:

> "Potential AAC users may believe that AAC reduces motivation to improve natural speech and delays language development."

ASHA treats this as an evidence-based professional issue, not a neutral open question. ASHA's position is consistent with their published research: AAC **supplements** (not replaces) communication, and early introduction (as early as 12 months) leads to the best outcomes across multiple domains of communication development.

**Citation:** ASHA Practice Portal. Augmentative and Alternative Communication.asha.org/Practice-Portal/professional-issues/augmentative-and-alternative-communication/

### The National Institute for Health Research (NIHR), United Kingdom

The NIHC Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects published a quality-assessed review of the Millar/Light/Schlosser study, confirming: "Increases in speech production were observed in 24 of 27 cases... In 10 of the 27 cases the PND was at least 90, indicating high levels of effectiveness of the intervention."

## The Bottom Line

The claim that "AAC delays speech" has not been supported by any credible scientific evidence. Every major study and every major professional organization says:

1. **AAC does not stop or delay speech development.**
2. **AAC can increase speech production** in many children, especially when introduced early.
3. **AAC provides immediate communication access**—something no child should be denied while "waiting to talk."
4. **The myth persists through misunderstanding of cause and effect,** provider bias, and historical lack of evidence (not current evidence).

### AAC does NOT mean: "I've given up on your child speaking."
### AAC means: "Your child deserves to communicate starting today."
