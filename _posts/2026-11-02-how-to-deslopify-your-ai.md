# Summary: How to Deslopify Your AI

[The AI Daily Brief - How to Deslopify Your AI](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Deslopify-Your-AI-e3rjbqk)

In this Operator's Cut, host Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW) and recurring guest Nufar Gaspar tackle the most practical problem of the agentic era: slop. If phase one of AI at work was learning to produce volumes of content -- a skill now democratized -- phase two is distinguishing yourself by using these tools well. The episode covers why machines produce slop, why humans tolerate it, how to spot it, and the habits that keep the work yours.

* **The working definition of slop**: work where the person who should have done the thinking skipped it, partly or entirely, and the cost moved to whoever received it. Merriam-Webster made "slop" its 2025 word of the year, defining it as low-quality digital content produced in quantity by AI -- and 2026's mass rollout of agentic tools turned the trickle into a flood.
* **The scale outside the office**: more than half of the music uploaded to Deezer every day is now fully AI-generated, yet AI tracks account for only 1-3% of what people actually listen to -- volume without demand.
* **Workslop by the numbers**: a Stanford Social Media Lab and BetterUp study found about 40% of US desk workers received workslop in the past month, each incident costing the receiver close to two hours; in Glean's survey of 6,000 workers, nearly 70% admitted shipping AI-generated work to someone else without ever reading it.
* **Why the machine produces slop**: models predict the most likely answer, so a generic question returns roughly the internet's average. Post-training compounds it -- Stanford researchers identified a "typicality bias" in which human raters reward familiar text, narrowing outputs into long, listy defaults, and the NeurIPS 2025 "Artificial Hivemind" paper found models from different companies converge on strikingly similar answers, so switching vendors won't buy you originality.
* **The three-model experiment**: Nufar gave the latest OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google models the same generic prompt in incognito mode -- write a LinkedIn post about how AI is changing marketing -- and got near-identical structure and content; rerunning it with her own point of view, experience, and style preferences produced a much better starting point.
* **Why humans tolerate slop**: the brain is a cognitive miser, pilots call the failure mode "automation complacency," and research shows the more people trust AI, the less critical thinking they apply. Slop comes from ease under pressure, from not knowing the craft well enough to ask well, and most troubling, from outsourcing the thinking itself.
* **Five questions to detect slop**: could the sender defend every sentence without rereading it? Did they read all of it before sending? Is there anything only this person could have contributed? Does it save the reader time or cost them time? And the best test: would you put your name on it in all capital letters?
* **Detectors and humanizers miss the point**: AI detectors measure surface tells like colon and bullet density, and "humanized" text still gets flagged about three-quarters of the time -- the reader's real complaint was always the missing human point of view, not the style.
* **NLW's split for his new show How We Got Here**: AI does all the research (reports of every length across multiple models), but the point of view and narrative architecture stay human -- his proof that AI can't own a perspective is asking it to steelman two opposing positions, which it argues each with apparent deepest conviction.
* **The honest cost of de-sloppifying**: each 6,000-word companion essay is drafted by AI from his raw talk-over transcript rather than an outline, then still takes 5-10 hours of paragraph-level rewriting in a CMS he built for the site -- keeping the quirks an English teacher would mark wrong because they sound like him.
* **Stage one of the playbook -- before opening the tool**: know what you think first, then brain-dump it by dictation and let the model find the structure; tell the model how confident you are and ask it to push back ("I'm about 60% sure the problem is onboarding -- challenge me hard"); and define the audience, desired outcome, and format standards up front.
* **Stage two -- shepherding execution**: ask for six directions that differ in approach (including borrowed from other domains) instead of accepting the first, most probable answer; avoid "do it end to end" delegation because a polished finished piece triggers sunk-cost thinking; decide review checkpoints in advance; and convene an AI "board" of reviewer personas, including a devil's advocate, before important work ships.
* **Respect the read budget**: never send something you wouldn't read yourself, or "two models end up talking while two people pretend to communicate" -- and choose the format on purpose, since AI makes decks, podcasts, HTML, and video equally cheap to produce.
* **Teach the model your taste**: show it your best work, work you admire, and work you dislike, ask it to profile what the good ones share, codify that as a reusable skill -- then deliberately break the mold when the audience, scope, or goal changes so sameness doesn't set in.
* **Leaders must name it**: when long, generic, clearly unread work lands on your desk, say so -- "send me your take in five sentences" -- and never allow slop in customer-facing work; Figma's rule requires labeling AI-written work "written with AI" at the top, and NLW notes the cost-transfer framing explains the visceral reaction: slop leaves the reader holding the work bag.

The core takeaway: as models improve, the generic answer gets more tempting and being an early AI adopter no longer differentiates anyone -- the premium now goes to work with a human fingerprint, and the differentiated skill is taste and intention: thinking before you open the tool, scaling effort to the stakes, and respecting other people's time as much as your own.
