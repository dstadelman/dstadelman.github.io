# Summary: AI Companies Still Haven't Delivered on Their Biggest Promises

[AI Daily Brief - AI Companies Still Haven't Delivered on Their Biggest Promises](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/AI-Companies-Still-Havent-Delivered-on-Their-Biggest-Promises-e3nhjtc)

Dario Amodei almost never posts, but a claim on the All-In podcast that he believes Anthropic might one day be the only private company left dragged the famously offline CEO into a rare appearance on X. In two long posts he argued that regulation is not automatically regulatory capture, that AI's structural risk is how power concentrates via scaling laws rather than via government rules, and that the public's distrust is a decades-old crisis of trust in institutions that no marketing campaign can fix [source](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-08-17). His most quoted line -- which doubles as the episode's title -- concedes that the fairest criticism of AI companies is that they haven't yet delivered on their big promises, and along the way the episode covers ZAI's GLM 5.3, Anthropic keeping its most capable unreleased model in-house, and a rumored $2 trillion IPO.

* **The All-In clip that started the firestorm:** Gavin Baker, CIO of Eutrades Capital, said on the All-In podcast that multiple credible people inside Anthropic told him Dario Amodei and co. believe that in the future there may be only Anthropic, governments, and everyone else -- a bet Baker said he'd take the under on, while David Sacks called the sentiment "hubristic" and "getting into SBF land."
* **Baker's real framing -- concentrate or distribute?:** Baker argued the core question is whether AI is too dangerous to concentrate in the hands of a few companies and politicians via regulation, or too dangerous to distribute broadly -- and said Dario's messaging had already "lost the argument," warning his good-faith push for regulation would end up fueling anti-data-center advocacy in America.
* **Dario's two-post defense:** Refusing the "concentrate or distribute" binary, he called the Silicon Valley shorthand "regulation = regulatory capture = concentration of power" an oversimplification that underrates "the decentralizing power of objective and fair institutional processes," cited Anthropic's support for California's SB 53 as deliberately exempting smaller competitors, and said he's supportive of the Trump administration's reported pre-deployment testing and Demis Hassabis's FINRA-like oversight idea.
* **Power concentrates from scaling laws, not rules:** Dario's structural argument is that AI tends to concentrate power for reasons rooted in "the extreme implications of the scaling laws," and that open weights don't solve this -- they just shift the concentration to whoever has the most compute and chips, roughly the frontier labs plus hardware providers.
* **The trust problem is the headline:** On messaging, Dario conceded that "saying that AI will cure cancer is more of a cliché than it is inspiring," and delivered the episode's title line -- that by far the most accurate criticism of AI companies, "including Anthropic," is that they haven't yet delivered on their big promises to benefit the world, noting Anthropic is now ramping up biology and medicine work and that honest messaging on risks is more credible than spinning them away.
* **Reactions were split:** The Information's Jessica Lessin said the two posts did what Dario had struggled to do all year -- changing his own narrative -- and Baker himself praised the "constructive, thoughtful" engagement, while critics including former AI alignment researcher Susan Zhang, X commentator Hader, and "Terminally Online" said he was backpedaling and, tellingly, never actually denied having made the "only private company" claim.
* **Why the two sides keep talking past each other:** PR strategist Lulu Cheng Meservey's read is that Dario defends himself quantitatively (I wrote one essay on benefits and one on risks) against a qualitative charge (your messaging feels negative), which is why both keep missing each other; Replit's Amjad Masad pushed back that scaling laws are "not laws of physics" but empirical relationships that change if you change the architecture, objective, or dataset; and OpenAI's Angel Brodin countered that pharma has delivered enormous health gains yet remains one of the least trusted industries, so people will judge AI companies on pricing, access, lobbying, and how gains are distributed -- not on breakthroughs alone.
* **GLM 5.3 squeezed from a mid-sized model:** ZAI's new GLM 5.3 is built on the GLM 5.2 base with gains from scaling reinforcement learning alone; it lands ~5 points behind the US frontier on Terminal Bench 3.0 (28.3%) and posts state-of-the-art results on agentic benchmarks like GDPVal, while costing well under the per-token price of Fable 5, GPT-5.6 Sol, and Kimi K3.
* **Anthropic's unreleased Model 2 stays internal:** A mid-July risk report disclosed an "Model 2" Anthropic describes as "somewhat more capable than Mythos 5" that the company has no plans to release; on Anthropic's internal AI R&D benchmark it scores 62.8% versus 50.3% for Mythos 5 -- and because the report is over a month old, the labs' current internal frontier is likely further still.
* **The $2 trillion IPO whisper number:** Anthropic has begun investor and bank meetings, telling them Q2 revenue hit $11.5 billion (up 14x year over year, ~$46 billion annualized) and investors told the Financial Times they expect a $2 trillion debut -- larger than SpaceX's debut and well above the May raise -- while Anthropic's own reported forecast is $190-200 billion in revenue by 2028.
* **Wall Street starts to poke holes in the math:** Fortune notes public stocks trade on earnings, not revenue, multiples, and at an average multiple a $2 trillion Anthropic would need annual profits of roughly $59-79 billion -- though Anthropic clearly isn't being valued like an average company, so the host expects the debate to keep building into the IPO.

The real story of this episode isn't the numbers or the benchmarks -- it's that when Dario Amodei was forced off his perch and into the public square, the single most quotable thing he produced was a concession, not a defense: AI companies haven't yet delivered on their biggest promises. That's where the trust problem actually sits. Everything else in the episode -- the GLM 5.3 benchmark, the Model 2 gap, the 14x growth -- is just the backdrop against which a company that is about to become one of the most important public companies in the world is being asked to do what pharma did for a century or, if it wants people to believe it, to finally do.
