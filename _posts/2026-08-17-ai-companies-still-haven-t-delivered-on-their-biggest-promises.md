# Summary: AI Companies Still Haven't Delivered on Their Biggest Promises

[AI Daily Brief - AI Companies Still Haven't Delivered on Their Biggest Promises](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/AI-Companies-Still-Havent-Delivered-on-Their-Biggest-Promises-e3nhjtc)

Dario Amodei almost never posts, but a claim on the All-In podcast that he believes Anthropic might one day be the only private company left dragged the famously offline CEO into a rare appearance on X. In two long posts he argued that regulation is not automatically regulatory capture, that AI's structural risk is how power concentrates via scaling laws rather than via government rules, and that the public's distrust is a decades-old crisis of trust in institutions that no marketing campaign can fix. His most quoted line -- which doubles as the episode's title -- concedes that the fairest criticism of AI companies is that they haven't yet delivered on their big promises, and along the way the episode covers ZAI's GLM 5.3, Anthropic keeping its most capable unreleased model in-house, and a rumored $2 trillion IPO.

* **The All-In clip that started the firestorm:** Gavin Baker, CIO of Atreides Management, said on the All-In podcast that multiple credible people told him Dario Amodei believes Anthropic could end up being the only private company in the world -- "in this vision, an Anthropic maximalist vision, there's Anthropic, and then there are governments, and that's it" [source](https://officechai.com/ai/claim-that-anthropic-wants-to-be-the-only-private-company-in-the-world-draws-ceo-dario-amodei-to-x-with-clarification/). David Sacks called the sentiment "so hubristic" and "getting into SBF land."
* **Baker's framing -- concentrate or distribute?:** Baker wrote up that the core question is whether AI is "too dangerous to concentrate" in the hands of a few companies and politicians via regulation, or "too dangerous to distribute" across many -- and sided with distribution, quoting Mark Zuckerberg's line that "the notion that AI is so dangerous that the only safe path is an extreme concentration of power seems inherently problematic." He argued that Dario's regulatory push had "already lost the argument" (noting "essentially every major company other than Anthropic has signed Jensen's [Huang] letter") and that his good-faith warnings had paradoxically ended up useful to anti-data-center advocacy groups in America.
* **Dario's two-post defense, part one -- regulation:** Refusing the "concentrate or distribute" binary, he called the Silicon Valley shorthand "regulation = regulatory capture = concentration of power" an oversimplification that underrates "the decentralizing power of objective and fair institutional processes," pointing to Anthropic's support for California's SB 53 and its ambivalence about SB 1047 -- both of which exempt smaller firms by revenue or training-cost threshold -- and to the testing regimes it has advocated for at CAISI and the White House, which scrutinize frontier models more strictly than challengers [source](https://officechai.com/ai/claim-that-anthropic-wants-to-be-the-only-private-company-in-the-world-draws-ceo-dario-amodei-to-x-with-clarification/).
* **Dario's two-post defense, part two -- the structural point:** AI tends to concentrate power for reasons rooted in "the extreme implications of the scaling laws," not in government rules, and open weights do not fix this; they just shift the concentration to whoever has the most compute in chips, which is roughly the frontier labs plus the hardware providers. He said he's supportive of the Trump administration's reported pre-deployment testing of frontier models and of Demis Hassabis's FINRA-like oversight idea.
* **The trust problem is the headline:** On messaging, Dario conceded that "saying that AI will cure cancer is more of a cliche than it is inspiring" and that "by far the most accurate criticism of AI companies, including Anthropic, is that we haven't yet delivered on our big promises to benefit the world. That is totally on us." He said Anthropic is ramping up biology and medicine work and expects "early glimmers" in the coming months, and that honest risk messaging is more credible than spinning it away.
* **The reactions split:** Baker himself praised the "constructive, thoughtful" engagement; The Information's Jessica Lessin said the two posts did for Dario what he'd struggled to do all year -- change his own narrative; and former alignment researcher Will DePuy said Dario "should be writing a lot more in public." On the other side, former OpenAI researcher Susan Zhang, AI commentator Hader, and the handle "Terminally Online" all called it backpedaling -- and Susan Zhang and Austin Allred both pointed out that Dario never actually denied having made the "only private company" claim [unverified-by-web attribution; as stated by the host].
* **Why the two sides keep missing each other:** PR strategist Lulu Cheng Meservey's read is that Dario answers a qualitative charge (your messaging is too negative) with a quantitative defense ("I wrote one essay on benefits and one on risks"), so the two keep talking past each other; Replit's Amjad Masad pushed back that scaling laws are "not laws of physics" but empirical relationships that can change if the architecture, objective, or dataset changes; OpenAI's Angel Brodin countered that pharma has delivered enormous health gains yet remains one of the least trusted industries -- people will judge AI companies on pricing, access, lobbying, and how gains are distributed, not on breakthroughs alone [unverified-by-web attribution; as stated by the host].
* **The host's own read:** For the host -- who agrees there is a real trust gap between the public and the labs that messaging alone won't close -- the core error is Dario claiming to understand that social media clips the worst parts and then continuing to do an "endless string of interviews" full of easily sound-bited negative statistics; public perception is not shaped by counting how many positive versus negative words he writes, it is shaped by the actual media environment an AI CEO has to operate in [source](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-08-17).
* **GLM 5.3 squeezed out of a mid-sized model:** ZAI (Zhipu AI)'s GLM 5.3 is built on the GLM 5.2 base with gains from scaling reinforcement learning alone; it posts ~28.3% on Terminal Bench 3.0 (roughly 5 points behind the US frontier of Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, 11 points ahead of Kimi K3), ~66.9% on DeepSWE (slightly behind Kimi K3), and state-of-the-art results on agentic / GDPVal-style benchmarks; per-token cost is well under a tenth of Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, and ZAI is taking a phased approach -- trusted-partner testing before publishing full weights. ZAI also said cyber performance was a key RL target and that during the Hugging Face incident, cyber defenders had been forced to fall back on GLM 5.2 because the guardrails on frontier models rendered them useless [unverified-by-web; as stated by the host and the episode page].
* **Nathan Lambert's broader point:** The open-model researcher used the release to argue that the industry should stop being surprised by Chinese labs -- "ZAI seems to have strength in post-training when compared to Kimi, which is more of a pre-training masterpiece"; the simpler explanation is that they are just very good at what they do, and writing them off as benchmark-maxers or distillers is a way of underestimating them [unverified-by-web attribution; as stated by the host].
* **The gap in the gap:** Anthropic's second risk report (dated 15 Jul) disclosed that as of mid-July they had three significant unreleased models in internal use -- Opus 5, an unreleased model roughly in line with Mythos 5, and "Model 2," which Anthropic describes as "somewhat more capable than Mythos 5" and for which they have "no plans for releasing ... publicly." X user ChrisGBT pointed out that on Anthropic's internal AI R&D benchmark, Model 2 scores 62.8% versus 50.3% for Mythos 5 and 54.8% for Mythos Preview -- meaning the actual frontier gap between what labs have internally and what's released to the public is wider than it has been, particularly now that government involvement means some frontier models are gated for the state [unverified-by-web attribution; as stated by the host].
* **OpenAI's Astra in the wind:** OpenAI staffers have started vague-posting about a model the host calls "Astra" (whatever the final name lands as), and the episode treats that as the next major family landing in users' hands.
* **The $2 trillion IPO whisper:** Anthropic has begun investor and investment-bank meetings; Anthropic reportedly told investors that revenue hit $11.5B in Q2 (up 14x year-over-year, ~$46B annualized) and that they expect revenue to reach $100-120B by the end of 2026, but investors told the Financial Times they expect a $2 trillion debut -- larger than SpaceX's debut this year and more than double the valuation at the May raise. Anthropic's own 2028 projection, per Reuters, is $190-200B [source](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026-08-17/anthropic-is-reportedly-aiming-for-a-valuation-of/).
* **Wall Street starting to poke holes in the math:** Fortune noted that public stocks trade on earnings multiples, not revenue multiples, and that at an average multiple, a $2 trillion Anthropic would need annual profits in the neighborhood of $59-79B to justify the price; the host expects this gap to keep growing as the IPO approaches.
* **Morningstar's counterpoint:** Morningstar analyst Malik Khan wrote that even if GLM 5.3 performs well, "all tailwinds to cloud infrastructure companies" remain intact because cheap models still need the same GPU fabric to run on [unverified-by-web attribution; as stated by the host].

The real story of this episode isn't the benchmarks or the IPO math -- it's that when Dario Amodei was forced off his perch into the public square, the single most quotable thing he produced was a concession, not a defense: AI companies haven't yet delivered on their biggest promises. That's where the trust problem actually sits. Everything else in the episode -- GLM 5.3, Model 2, the 14x revenue growth, the $2 trillion whisper -- is the backdrop against which a company about to join one of the most important public companies in the world is being asked to do what it has not yet done: deliver.
